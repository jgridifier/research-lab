The short version

  • Plans only. This is the first edition of a running file. It covers the four methods already run on 11-10 and 11-11. No new fits were run, nothing is being built, nothing is promoted, and no APPROVAL.md is written. Every number is from those scout pages, their run JSON, or the analysis-skills RESULTS files. Later daily scouts should append new methods in the same form. The full text, including citations, is in secret-sauce.md.
  • Exactly three avenues per method, always in this order, as a row of three cards: (1) Engineering — port, speed, or scale; (2) Math — a new estimator, null, or inference; (3) New use on our data — a novel application tied to a research question. Each card has a one-line pitch, effort (S = days, M = one to a few weeks, L = a substantial port or a paper-scale change), and the headline test. The top pick has an amber badge. Detail sits below the cards.
  • Forecasting is lower priority. Jared’s own model framework is at v2.1, and it moved past MARSS-style state-space models at v0.1. MARSS is treated here as a v0.1-era tool for decomposing sticky versus market-driven share and visualizing that split, not as a forecasting upgrade. Its three avenues stay off forecasting improvements.
  • Found via for each method is only what the 11-10 and 11-11 pages, run folders and watchlist actually recorded: the channel, the exact link, and the date seen. If a paper URL was not recorded, the line says so.

Method 01

changeforest

Londschien, Bühlmann & Kovács (2023), JMLR 24(216):1–45 · Rust core, v1.2.1 · niche · 11-10 scout · RESULTS.md · recommended, awaiting approval — not promoted

Found via. PyPI and crates.io, seen 2026-11-10. Recorded links: https://pypi.org/project/changeforest/, https://crates.io/crates/changeforest, and https://github.com/mlondschien/changeforest (the watchlist’s link). The 11-10 footer also records scanning the JMLR (2023) paper; an exact paper URL was not recorded. An X search that day is recorded as turning up only noise (the name collision was with “partykit”).

The method finds volatility and correlation breaks that a mean-change test misses (0 mean breaks vs 35 distributional breaks on weekly changes; 28 daily breaks, including the 2022 stock–rate correlation flip). It is already fast (9,174 × 4 daily rows in 1.2 s) and already in Rust, and it returns 0 breaks on iid-shuffled data. The weakness is the independence assumption. On weekly changes, a stationary VAR(1) null (spectral radius 0.957, no breaks by construction) produced 20–37 breaks in 5 simulations — as many as the real data (35; 32–47 across seeds). Prewhitening, already tested in RESULTS, left 12 stable breaks and 0–2 on the same null. Daily changes already pass (radius 0.50, 0 breaks).

Time-series mode in the crate

Bake persistence check, VAR(1) prewhitening, 10-seed consensus and the VAR null into the crate as a first-class time-series mode. Not a port — the Rust core is already there.

Test. Parity with the hand-rolled RESULTS (12 stable prewhitened breaks; 0–2 on the VAR null), then speed of that whole pipeline.
2 · MathTop pick

Block-permutation test of the gain

Replace the iid permutation of the classifier gain with a block-permutation test, so a break has to beat residual autocorrelation, not just a shuffle. Effort L.

Test. On VAR(1) surrogates with radius 0.957 and no breaks, the false-break rate is near a pre-registered α (original: 20–37 breaks, as many as the real weekly series).
3 · New use on our dataEffort S

Regime-timed sticky share, for Q1

Use the stable market-regime dates (including the 2022 correlation flip) to ask whether Q1 sticky-share increments cluster at those dates.

Test. Mean absolute MARSS sticky increment in a window around those dates, versus a circular-shift null of the sticky series.

1 · Engineering, in detail

The original keeps a split at \(s\) if a permutation test of the classifier gain \(G(s)\) rejects. This avenue does not change \(G\). It makes mandatory, inside the crate, the pipeline we already had to hand-roll: refuse persistent levels; prewhiten when the VAR(1) radius is high; return only breaks that recur in at least 8 of 10 seeds; always report a stationary VAR(1) null (the iid shuffle passed on weekly changes even where the VAR null failed). Closest prior: Londschien, Bühlmann & Kovács (2023) already ship the forest and an iid permutation, in Rust. We did not find a first-class time-series mode that bundles the rest. Meinshausen & Bühlmann (2010), JRSS-B 72(4):417–473, is the closest formal frame for the multi-seed core. Weekly changes failed the VAR null until we prewhitened by hand; seed counts moved between 32 and 47, which is why the 11-10 verdict was “interesting.” Parity test: recover the RESULTS numbers (12 prewhitened weekly core breaks, 0–2 on the residual VAR null, 28 daily dates with 0 VAR-null breaks) on the same panels, then compare speed for “10 seeds + VAR null.”

2 · Math, in detail (top pick)

The original permutation shuffles rows, which is valid only if rows are exchangeable. On weekly changes they are not (radius 0.957). Prewhitening fixes size empirically, but it looks for breaks in innovations and can miss a break that is a change in the autoregressive structure. The math keeps \(G(s)\) on the original (differenced) series and changes the test: permute blocks of length \(L\) long enough to cover the short-run dependence, and keep a split only if \(G(s)\) exceeds the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of those block-permuted gains. That is Kirch (2007), “Block permutation principles for the change analysis of dependent data,” Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference 137:2453–2474, applied to the classifier gain rather than to a CUSUM. Zeileis & Hothorn (2013), Statistical Papers 54(4):931–954, discuss block permutations for structural-change tests and point to Kirch. We did not find a paper that applies this to the 2023 classifier gain. We would still not run on undifferenced near-unit-root levels (radius 0.997). Headline weekly failure: 20–37 breaks on a no-break VAR null, against 35 on the real data. Test: 200 simulated weekly series from the fitted VAR(1); the fraction with at least one break is at most a pre-registered \(\alpha\) (for example 0.10). On the real weekly series, recover at least 8 of the 12 stable prewhitened dates RESULTS already trusts (including 2007-07, 2008-10, 2019-12, 2020-06, 2022-01), at the 11-10 tolerance of ±13 weeks. On daily changes, where the original already has 0 VAR-null breaks, do not invent a crop of breaks, and keep a pre-registered fraction of the 23 seed-stable daily breaks.

3 · New use, in detail

Aimed at Q1. Take the stable market-regime dates and test whether MARSS sticky-state increments from the 11-11 PROXY dealer-share panels cluster at those dates more than under a circular shift of the sticky series. Descriptive only; it runs none of the pre-registered Y-9C forecast trials. We did not find a paper that uses classifier-based distributional regimes as event times for a compositional share decomposition. The circular-shift null is the same family already used on 11-11. Test: for GS and JPM equity-revenue sticky increments (and, separately, the foreign-IHC trading-asset group), mean absolute increment in a ±1-quarter window around market-regime dates, versus 1,000 circular shifts; one-sided \(p < 0.05\), BH-adjusted across the pre-registered pairs. A negative result is also a result. No forecast metric. Math is the top pick because the package is already fast and already in Rust; the thing that changed what we believed on weekly data was a failed dependence null.

Method 02

partykit · ctree and MOB

Hothorn, Hornik & Zeileis (2006), JCGS 15(3):651–674 · Zeileis, Hothorn & Hornik (2008), JCGS 17(2):492–514 · Hothorn & Zeileis (2015), JMLR 16:3905–3909 · partykit 1.2-29, C core via libcoin · well-known · R-only · 11-10 scout · RESULTS.md · recommended (scoped), awaiting approval — not promoted

Found via. CRAN, https://cran.r-project.org/package=partykit, seen 2026-11-10. The 11-10 footer records scanning “CRAN (partykit and Zeileis-group packages)” and the JCGS (2006, 2008) and JMLR (2015) papers; exact paper URLs were not recorded. An X search the same day “turned up only noise… because ‘partykit’ collides with an unrelated web framework.”

The trees are readable and fast (300 block-bootstrap refits in 3.6 s). MOB is the best 11-10 result: one NFCI split at 0.057, the VIX→credit-spread slope roughly doubles (0.0293 → 0.0587), calendar time does not split (\(p = 0.83\)), BIC −350.5 → −513.6. As a forecaster of 6-month spread changes, ctree lost to the historical mean (OOS \(R^2 = -0.31\)); we are not proposing to fix that. The weakness is inference on time series. A circularly shifted response still produced a ctree split in 60% of 100 shifts. For MOB, circularly shifting the partitioning variables still produced a split in 53%. The county cross-section is fine (iid-permuted response split in 0% and 4% of 50 permutations).

1 · EngineeringEffort L

Faithful Python or Rust port

A faithful port of ctree and MOB, with R partykit 1.2-29 as the oracle for parity, then speed. This is the textbook engineering sauce: the package is R-only.

Test. Split variables, thresholds, leaf counts and MOB coefficients match the three 11-10 applications; then speed versus 3.6 s / 13.5 s in R.
2 · MathTop pick

Dependence-aware split tests

Block-permutation linear statistics for ctree, and a MOB fluctuation test that stays size-correct when the state variable is persistent. Effort L.

Test. Circular-shift false-split rate near a pre-registered α (original: 60% for ctree, 53% for MOB), while the county trees and the real NFCI MOB split still appear.
3 · New use on our dataEffort S

MOB of Q1 market loadings

A MOB of Q1 dealer share–market loadings, partitioned by NFCI or VIX, to see whether the market part of the share decomposition itself changes with conditions.

Test. A split that clears a circular-shift null of the partitioning variables, with a better description (for example BIC) than constant loadings. No forecast RMSE.

1 · Engineering, in detail

The same estimators as the R package: ctree’s linear statistic \(T_j\) and Strasser–Weber permutation moments, computed today in C via libcoin; MOB’s node model and M-fluctuation test (Zeileis 2005). Faithful means that, against frozen partykit 1.2-29, splits, thresholds, leaf membership and node coefficients match to a pre-registered tolerance. Speed comes after parity. A search turned up Python packages that implement related permutation-based trees; we did not find a port with a documented parity suite against partykit on real panels, and we are not treating any of those as an oracle-tested port. Test: the same comparison as RESULTS (default absolute tolerance \(10^{-4}\); exact match on split variable, published threshold rounding, leaf \(n\) and rules) on the three 11-10 applications. Application 3’s CV scores keep the documented RNG-fold exception. Then speed: 300 block-bootstrap refits of application 1, and lmtree on the 30,740-row replicated county table, versus the recorded R times (3.6 s and 13.5 s).

2 · Math, in detail (top pick)

For ctree, replace exchangeable-row permutation moments with a block permutation of the influence functions, in the sense of Kirch (2007) and Zeileis & Hothorn (2013). For MOB, two layers. (i) Serially correlated scores: partykit can take a sandwich covariance (Zeileis 2005; the package suggests sandwich). We did not use that on 11-10. The first, small experiment is to rerun application 2 with a HAC covariance and repeat the 100 circular shifts of the partitioning variables. (ii) If that false-split rate stays near 53%, the gap is a persistent state variable lining up with volatility clustering. Hansen (2000), “Testing for structural change in conditional models,” Journal of Econometrics 97(1):93–115, is the closest prior: standard break tests have the wrong distribution when covariates are persistent. The sauce is a split test whose critical values come from circular shifts (or a Hansen-style fixed-regressor bootstrap) of the partitioning variables, so “NFCI is persistent and volatility clusters” is in the null. We did not find a paper that puts that calibration inside partykit’s variable-selection step. Test: 100 circular shifts; false-split rates at most a pre-registered \(\alpha\) (for example 0.10), against 60% and 53%. On unshifted data, MOB should still split on NFCI (published threshold 0.057, with a stated tolerance), and the county trees should match the original roots under the iid null that already passed. Math is the top pick: a faster port would only reproduce the 60% and 53% rates more quickly.

3 · New use, in detail

The 11-11 MARSS decomposition assumed constant market loadings \(D\). Window splits already showed that is not a fixed property of a firm: JPM’s equity-revenue share was 88% sticky / 12% market in 2011–17 and 2% sticky / 78% market in 2018–26. The new use is a MOB for the PROXY share panels, \(\Delta y_t = \beta_0 + \beta^\top d_t + \varepsilon_t\), partitioned by NFCI, VIX and calendar time, with a circular-shift null of the partitioning variables from the start. It is a description of when the market part of the share decomposition changes, not a forecast. Closest prior: Zeileis, Hothorn & Hornik (2008) is the method; we did not find this application to a market-versus-sticky share decomposition. Test: whether a split is declared, and on which variable; the circular-shift false-split rate must be at most a pre-registered \(\alpha\) before the real split is read; a descriptive comparison (BIC, or a leaf-wise variance split) against the constant-\(D\) MARSS M1 already published. No out-of-sample forecast RMSE, and none of the Y-9C trading-OT forecast trials.

Method 03

MARSS

Holmes, Ward & Wills (2012), The R Journal 4(1):11–19 · MARSS 3.11.10 · niche, from ecology · R-only · 11-11 scout, aimed at Q1, PROXY Y-9C · recommend for promote workflow — not started, not promoted

Found via. CRAN, https://cran.r-project.org/package=MARSS, seen 2026-11-11 (watchlist “Date seen”, and the 11-11 citation). The 11-11 page cites the R Journal (2012) article; an exact journal URL was not recorded. An X search that day is recorded as finding “no method leads and no data.” A “sources scanned” list like the 11-10 footer was not recorded for 11-11.

v0.1-era, not a forecasting upgrade. Jared’s own model framework is at v2.1, and it moved past MARSS-style state-space models at v0.1. The three avenues below treat MARSS as a v0.1-era tool for decomposing sticky versus market-driven share and for visualizing that split. They are deliberately not forecasting improvements — no new nowcast, no walk-forward RMSE target, no claim to beat a random walk. The 11-11 page already reported a mixed one-step comparison and made “no forecasting claim”; we leave that as is.

On the equity-revenue PROXY (62 quarters), market covariates explain 74% (BAC), 62% (MS) and 59% (JPM) of quarterly share-change variance, but only 22% for GS. GS’s fall from 52.3% to 23.1% of big-4 equity trading revenue sits almost entirely in the sticky state, and GS’s sticky increments mirror JPM’s (correlation −0.79, BH \(q = 0.038\)). On trading assets (14 dealers, 40 quarters), net shifts are almost all sticky: foreign-bank IHCs’ share fell from 13.8% to 5.9%. The weakness is sample length for labels: AICc can tell sticky from slow mean reversion at 62 quarters (false “sticky” on stationary AR(1) surrogates 0–18%; power 57–65%) but not reliably at 40 quarters (false “sticky” 15–65%). Decompositions also shift across windows. Univariate clr fits ignore the sum-to-zero link between dealers.

1 · EngineeringEffort L

Faithful port, for the decomposition

A faithful Python or Rust port of the MARSS Kalman path, with R MARSS 3.11.10 as the oracle, aimed at fast decompositions, persistence nulls, and visualization — not at forecasting.

Test. Smoothed sticky/market/transient paths, variance splits, AICc choices and net-change attributions match the 11-11 panels; then speed.
2 · MathTop pick

A short-sample test for the sticky label

Replace AICc as the sticky-versus-mean-reverting label with a finite-sample bootstrap likelihood-ratio test that is calibrated at 40 quarters. For the decomposition, not a forecast. Effort M.

Test. At \(T=40\), the false “sticky” rate on stationary AR(1) surrogates drops from 15–65% into the \(T=62\) range (0–18%), with power on true random walks still at least 50%.
3 · New use on our dataEffort M

Joint “who took from whom,” and a picture

A joint compositional model with a structured process covariance, so “who took share from whom” is a parameter, with a visualization of the joint sticky paths.

Test. For GS↔JPM, the estimated sticky-increment association has an interval that excludes the clr built-in −0.333, against a circular-shift null; plus a readable joint chart.

1 · Engineering, in detail

The 11-11 model is \(x_t = b\, x_{t-1} + w_t\), \(y_t = x_t + D^\top d_t + v_t\), with a smoothed split of each path into sticky, market and transient parts. The port implements the same Kalman likelihood and the same smoothed states, with R MARSS 3.11.10 as oracle. After parity, speed is used to make the matched-persistence null and the charts cheap. It is not a forecasting library and would not be tested as one. Closest existing software, not a port: KFAS (Helske 2017, JSS 78(10):1–39), already on the 11-11 watchlist; Python statsmodels UnobservedComponents. We did not find a faithful Python or Rust port of MARSS with an oracle parity suite. Test: AICc, estimated \(b\), variance splits, net-change attributions and the published sticky-increment correlations, within a tight tolerance on the 11-11 panels (public PROXY, no raw private rows). Then speed of the decomposition-plus-null workload versus the recorded R time. Walk-forward RMSE is not a success criterion.

2 · Math, in detail (top pick)

AICc picked “sticky” or “slow mean reversion” as if that label were reliable. At 40 quarters it is not. The sauce replaces the AICc decision with a finite-sample test of the hypothesis that matters for the decomposition: is the sticky state a random walk at this \(T\)? A parametric bootstrap likelihood-ratio: fit the stationary model, simulate many series of the same length, and label a dealer “sticky” only if the real LR exceeds a pre-registered quantile of that bootstrap. Closest prior: Morley, Panovska & Sinclair (2017), “Testing Stationarity with Unobserved-Components Models,” Macroeconomic Dynamics 21(1):160–182, who found that a bootstrap LR in unobserved-components models has much better small-sample behaviour than standard LM tests. Nyblom & Harvey (2000), Econometric Theory 16(2):176–199, is the classical LM ancestor. Our 11-11 analysis 1d is already a Monte Carlo of AICc’s error rate; the contribution is to make a calibrated test the formal rule. This is the caveat already on the 11-11 page: “below about 60 quarters, sticky vs slow mean reversion can’t be told apart.” The 14-dealer panel is 40 quarters. Test: the same 1d design (40 stationary AR(1) series and 40 true random walks per dealer). At \(T=40\), the false “sticky” rate must fall into the 0–18% band we already treat as acceptable at \(T=62\) (or at most 0.20, pre-registered). Power on a true random walk stays at least 0.50 (11-11 powers were 50–80% at \(T=40\)). No forecast RMSE. Math is the top pick because the sentence we cannot yet stand behind on the 40-quarter panel is a single-firm “sticky” label.

3 · New use, in detail

Univariate clr fits “ignore the sum-to-zero link between dealers. A joint MARSS with a structured \(Q\) is the next step” — that is a quote from the 11-11 limitations. Estimate a joint state for the clr (or ilr) vector, with a process covariance \(Q\) that is the “who took share from whom” parameter, and plot the joint sticky paths (GS vs JPM; foreign-IHC versus US-dealer contrast). The GS↔JPM correlation (−0.79, BH \(q = 0.038\)) was computed post-hoc on univariate states; here it is estimated with uncertainty. Not a forecast. Closest prior: Snyder, Ord, Koehler, McLaren & Beaumont (2017), “Forecasting compositional time series: A state space approach,” International Journal of Forecasting 33(2):502–512, put Gaussian state-space models on log-ratio market shares. Their aim was forecasting shares. We would use the same compositional idea for decomposition and visualization only, with the 11-11 circular-shift null retained. Test: on E4 first (62 quarters), the estimated GS↔JPM association has an interval that excludes the clr built-in level of about −0.333, and a circular-shift p-value that still survives BH among the pre-registered pairs. The joint sticky-path chart’s end-points match the published net sticky changes (GS −0.99, JPM +0.79 in clr). A joint model that drops the off-diagonal of \(Q\) should recover the univariate variance splits. No forecast comparison. If the interval does not exclude −0.333, that is a reported failure of the “GS losses went to JPM” reading, not a reason to change the metric.

Method 04

InspectChangepoint

Wang & Samworth (2018), JRSS-B 80(1):57–83 · InspectChangepoint 1.2, last CRAN release recorded as 2022-05 · pure R · obscure · 11-11 scout, Q1 PROXY · interesting — not recommended for promote on that day’s evidence

Found via. CRAN, https://cran.r-project.org/package=InspectChangepoint, seen 2026-11-11 (watchlist “Date seen”, and the 11-11 citation). The 11-11 page cites the JRSS-B (2018) article; an exact journal URL was not recorded. An X search that day is recorded as finding “no method leads and no data.” A “sources scanned” list like the 11-10 footer was not recorded for 11-11.

The selling point is the “who moved” vector, and the method is very fast (9.6 ms per 14 × 40 panel). The weakness is persistence. On market-adjusted log-ratio levels it found 8 breaks in the trading-asset panel, while AR(1)-matched and random-walk surrogates with the same persistence averaged 12.39 and 13.28 (\(P(\ge\text{observed}) = 1.000\)). Those are not evidence of discrete events. On first differences (a change in drift, the right test for a migration that starts or stops), there were 0 breaks in trading assets and 1 non-significant break in revenue shares (after 2011Q3, GS-loaded; iid and block nulls \(p = 0.24\) and \(0.22\)). Read with MARSS: gradual persistent drift, not a handful of shocks.

Faithful port, null built in

A faithful Python or Rust port, with R InspectChangepoint 1.2 as the oracle, and a built-in persistence-matched null as default output. Speed is not the 11-11 pain point at this panel size.

Test. Break dates and projection vectors match the 11-11 panels; the persistence null is reported on every run.

Inspect valid under persistence, and for a change in drift

A sparse-projection procedure whose critical values are valid under near-unit-root persistence, and that can look for a change in drift, not only a change in mean. Effort L.

Test. Size near a pre-registered α on the same AR(1)/RW surrogates of panel A (original: those surrogates produce more breaks than the data); power on a planted sparse shift or slope change.
3 · New use on our dataEffort M

“Who moved” at Q1 trend-change dates

Use the “who moved” vector as a readout at trend-change dates for the foreign-IHC retreat and the GS→JPM shift, after a persistence null is passed.

Test. Dates and named dealers for those two migrations, clearing a persistence null, compared to Narrowest-Over-Threshold as a baseline.

1 · Engineering, in detail

The published procedure forms a CUSUM matrix, estimates a sparse projection, and recurses with wild binary segmentation. The port reproduces that, with R 1.2 as oracle. At 9.6 ms in pure R, speed is not the pain point; a default persistence-matched null (the 11-11 2b/2c surrogates) is. Without that default, the face-value 8 breaks are easy to over-read; 11-11 2a told those stories and 2b took them back. We did not find a faithful Python or Rust port with an oracle parity suite. Test: on panels A and E4, break dates and the published top projection loadings match InspectChangepoint 1.2. The default output of a single call must include the 2b comparison, and those means must match the published 0.76 / 12.39 / 13.28 (panel A levels) at the same seed, to a stated Monte-Carlo tolerance.

2 · Math, in detail (top pick)

Two related modifications. (i) Size under persistence. Wang & Samworth (2018) also discuss an extension to short-range dependent Gaussian processes. Our share levels have lag-1 persistence of about 1, which is not short-range dependence, and that is where the test broke (8 vs 12.4–13.3). The sauce is a sparse-projection CUSUM whose critical values (or a prewhitened / long-run-variance studentization) are valid for local-to-unity or unit-root panels of this shape — or a default that refuses to report level breaks unless they beat the persistence-matched surrogate distribution we already computed. Kirch (2007) is a candidate for short-range errors; it is not, by itself, a solution at a unit root. (ii) A change in drift. Sticky migration is a change in slope, not a jump in the mean. The 11-11 run already applied Inspect to first differences and found nothing significant. The improvement is to put a piecewise-linear model inside the sparse-projection step, so \(\hat v\) is the direction of a drift change, with a test that has the right size under the observed negative autocorrelation of differences (median lag-1 about −0.43). Closest “when” method, without a “who” vector: Narrowest-Over-Threshold (Baranowski, Chen & Fryzlewicz 2019, JRSS-B 81(3):649–672), already on the 11-11 watchlist. We did not find a sparse-projection “who moved” method with proven size at near-unit-root persistence, or a published Inspect extension to slope changes. Test: reuse the 11-11 surrogate design. On AR(1) and RW surrogates of panel A, the false-discovery rate at a stated \(\alpha\) is at most 0.10. Plant a sparse mean or slope change of a pre-registered size on the order of the face-value 2018Q4 moves (JPM +1.82 pp, MS −1.23 pp, GS +1.02 pp — as a planted alternative, not as a claimed real break) and require, for example, power of at least 0.80 to flag a break within ±1 quarter and to load on the planted dealers. A calibrated non-rejection on the real first-differenced panels is acceptable. Math is the top pick: a port of a test that is invalid on our share levels would only reproduce the 8-versus-12.4 artifact more conveniently.

3 · New use, in detail

Once a dependence-valid trend-change procedure exists (avenue 2, or NOT for the dates plus Inspect as a projection readout), apply it to the Q1 PROXY to date the start or end of two already-described gradual migrations — the foreign-IHC retreat (13.8% → 5.9%) and the GS→JPM equity-revenue shift (52.3% → 23.1%, sticky correlation −0.79) — and to name the dealers in the projection. A visualization and a “who” on a trend, not a forecast, and not a mean-jump claim. Test: the persistence or slope-change null must pass before dates are read; if a date is declared, at least two of the top four projection loadings should match the dealers MARSS already named (foreign IHCs and JPM/GS on assets; GS and JPM on E4). NOT’s dates, if any, are a “when” baseline. A calibrated “no date” is a pass of the procedure and would agree with 11-11 2c. No forecast metric.
