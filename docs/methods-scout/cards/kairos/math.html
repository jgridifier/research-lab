Assumptions

  • Each type comes in and goes out of fashion once (unimodal over time: a "battleship" shape).
  • The type list is fixed across deposits.
  • Deposits are independent (used by the event model's intervals).

The statistics

Seriation by correspondence analysis. Take the SVD of the standardised residuals and order months and segments by their axis-1 coordinates:

$$S = D_r^{-1/2}\,(P - r c^{\top})\,D_c^{-1/2} = U\Sigma V^{\top},\qquad F = D_r^{-1/2}U\Sigma .$$

Event date (Bellanger & Husi): regress the known dates on the leading CA row coordinates; any mix, including a new month, then gets a date.

$$\hat t_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{k} \beta_k F_{ik}.$$

EPPM seriograph (Desachy): plot each share's excess over its column mean, \(\max(0,\ p_{ij}/r_i - \bar s_j)\), so hand-offs between types stand out.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · what CA finds\(S\) measures how far each month's mix is from the average mix, scaled so that \(\sum S_{ij}^2\) is the total inertia (the χ² statistic over \(n\)). Its first singular vector is the single direction of mix change that explains the most inertia, here 69.3%.
Step 2 · why a fashion cycle lands on axis 1If types rise and fall in sequence, nearby months share types and distant months share few. That gives a "horseshoe" in CA, whose first axis orders deposits along the sequence (the classical seriation result). Ordering by \(F_{i1}\) therefore recovers time up to sign: our sign is negative (Spearman −0.964).
Step 3 · dating a new monthA new mix needs no refit: its coordinate is its profile times the standard column coordinates (the CA transition formula), \(f = \sum_j (n_j/n_\cdot)\,v_{j1}/\sqrt{c_j}\). Plug \(f\) into the event model.
Step 4 · the null (Porčić)After seriation, count the modes (local peaks) in each type's sequence and sum. Under the seriation model the total is small. Compare it with 1,000 random month orders; p = share of random orders with as few modes.

Inference used for the Q1.4 link: correlation of mix speed with Form PF borrowing growth (overlapping 12-month changes), with a circular-shift null that keeps persistence, and Holm over 18 tests.

Toy example (computed on the box)

Three months, three segments, counts \(\begin{pmatrix}60&30&10\\40&40&20\\20&30&50\end{pmatrix}\). Shares by month: (.6,.3,.1), (.4,.4,.2), (.2,.3,.5); column means (.400, .333, .267).

  • EPPM: excesses are .200 for segment A in month 1, .067 for B in month 2, .233 for C in month 3, and 0 elsewhere: a clean hand-off A → B → C.
  • CA: axis 1 holds 94.7% of inertia; month coordinates −0.446, −0.106, +0.553, already in calendar order.
  • Event model: dating the months 2018, 2020, 2022 gives \(\hat t = 2020 + 3.873\,F_1\), fitted 2018.27, 2019.59, 2022.14.
  • A new mix (50, 35, 15): coordinate −0.276, so event date ≈ 2018.9: it "looks like" late 2018.
How it differs from the standard tool. The usual view is a stacked-area chart of shares, or a compositional PCA on log-ratios. Neither assumes a direction in time, so neither gives a date or a speed. Seriation adds a strong, testable structure (each type has one rise and fall) and turns the mix into a single clock. The cost: the structure can be wrong (coefficient 0.52 here, so some segments go up and down more than once), and the method is blind to a change in the type list.
