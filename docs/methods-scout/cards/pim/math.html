The model

$$P\big(Y_i \preccurlyeq Y_j \mid X_i, X_j\big) = \operatorname{expit}\!\big((X_j - X_i)^\top \beta\big),\qquad (i,j)\in\mathcal I .$$

The left side is the probabilistic index (PI): the chance that bank \(j\), which is higher by \(X_j - X_i\) on the stress metric, has the higher payout. \(\beta = 0\) gives PI = 0.5, "the metric does not order payouts". Nothing is assumed about the shape of \(Y\)'s distribution: only this pairwise probability is modelled (semiparametric).

The estimator

Make one pseudo-observation per pair, \(I_{ij} = \mathbf 1[Y_i < Y_j] + \tfrac12\mathbf 1[Y_i = Y_j]\) with design \(Z_{ij} = X_j - X_i\), and solve the estimating equation

$$U(\beta)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal I} Z_{ij}\Big(I_{ij} - \operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\beta)\Big) = 0 .$$

That is exactly the score of a logistic regression on the pairs, so it is fast (49,500 pairs fit in 0.05 s). But the pairs share banks, so they are not independent: the variance needs a sandwich that accounts for the overlap.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · unbiased estimating equationUnder the model, \(\mathbb E[I_{ij}\mid X_i,X_j] = \operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\beta)\), so each term of \(U(\beta)\) has mean zero at the true \(\beta\). Solving \(U(\hat\beta)=0\) is consistent even though the pairs are dependent.
Step 2 · it contains the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney testWith one binary covariate (group A = 0, B = 1) every cross-group pair has \(Z_{ij} = 1\), and \(U(\beta)=0\) gives \(\operatorname{expit}(\hat\beta) = \tfrac1{n_An_B}\sum_{i\in A,\,j\in B} I_{ij}\): the Mann–Whitney probability. A PIM is "the rank test turned into a regression"; with more covariates it gives partial effects (drawdown with pre-tax income held fixed).
Step 3 · converting to "per IQR"Because the link is on the difference, a gap of \(\Delta\) in the metric gives PI \(=\operatorname{expit}(\beta\Delta)\). Our pre-tax income fit has PI 0.606 per 1 pp, so \(\beta = \operatorname{logit}(0.606) = 0.431\), and at the IQR \(\Delta = 1.8\) pp: \(\operatorname{expit}(0.431\times1.8) = 0.685\), the 68% on the card. Drawdown: \(\operatorname{expit}(\operatorname{logit}(0.460)\times2.5) = 0.401\).
Step 4 · why outliers barely matterEach pair contributes \(I_{ij}\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\). Moving one bank's payout from 5% to 500% of RWA changes nothing unless it changes who beats whom. OLS instead squares the distance, so a near-zero net income can dominate.

Inference and the null

  • Pim's sandwich treats firm-years as independent, but firms recur across 13 years. We used a firm-block bootstrap (299 resamples of whole firms) for intervals and a within-year permutation of \(Y\) (499) for p-values. Within-year permutation keeps each year's payout norms and breaks the link to \(X\).
  • Small samples (18–35 banks a year) are where Amorim et al. (2018) found asymptotic PIM inference unreliable, which is why we lean on resampling.
  • 29 models in three families; Holm within each family.
Toy example (computed on the box)

Two banks with a weak stress result (A) paid 1.0 and 2.0; three with a strong result (B) paid 1.5, 3.0 and 4.0. Of the 6 A–B pairs, B pays more in 5 (only 2.0 > 1.5 goes the other way). So \(\widehat{PI} = 5/6 = 0.83\) and \(\hat\beta = \operatorname{logit}(0.83) = 1.61\). Change the 4.0 to 400: still 5 of 6, PI unchanged. An OLS slope would jump.

How it differs from the standard tool. OLS with year fixed effects models \(\mathbb E[Y\mid X]\) and reports a slope in payout units; with heavy tails it is driven by a few extreme ratios (−43% after trimming 2% of rows). A PIM models only the ordering within a year and reports a probability. Compared with the 11-14 IDR (full conditional distributions under an order constraint), it gives a partial effect size, at the cost of saying nothing about levels.
