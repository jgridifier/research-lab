Inputs and preprocessing

  • Fed public_results_DFAST_2026.csv (all scenarios), FR Y-9C HI-A Q4 payouts 2012–2025 (BHCK4460, 4598, 4783, 4782, 4340), SCB from the Large Bank Capital Requirements PDFs. The lab's verified copies from the 11-14 run.
  • Payout = common and preferred dividends plus net buybacks over RWA, calendar year \(Y\), for test year \(Y\); 360 firm-years. Next-year payouts as a timing check.
  • IQRs used for "per IQR": drawdown 2.5 pp, pre-tax income 1.8 pp, SCB 2.0 pp, gap 1.1 pp.

What we ran

  1. Sanity: pim on its own example data reproduces the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney index exactly.
  2. Q3.1/Q3.2: one-covariate and joint (drawdown + pre-tax income) PIMs, all years and SCB era.
  3. Q3.4: Adverse vs severely adverse gap on 2014–2019 (177 firm-years with both scenarios).
  4. Q3.5: dividends/RWA, net buybacks/RWA and dividend share, before vs after 2020.
  5. Robustness: trim the 8 most extreme payout ratios and refit both OLS and PIM.
  6. Inference: 299 firm-block bootstraps and 499 within-year permutations per model; Holm within families. All 29 models ran in about 3 minutes.
How to read the main chart (forest plot)
  1. Each row is one model; the dot is the PI per one-IQR difference in the stress metric.
  2. The vertical line at 0.5 means "no ordering". Right of it: the better-placed bank tends to pay more.
  3. Bars are firm-block bootstrap 95% intervals; a bar that crosses 0.5 is not distinguishable from no ordering.
  4. Compare "alone" with "joint" rows: drawdown alone sits at 0.40, but with pre-tax income in the model it moves to 0.50. Pre-tax income carries the ordering.
What it means for a decision-maker

Within a stress-test year, the bank whose severely adverse projected pre-tax income looks better usually pays out more: about 2 times in 3 for a typical gap. The capital drawdown on its own seems to matter only because it travels with pre-tax income. The buffer itself (SCB) shows no link. This is a description of how payouts lined up with published stress numbers, not advice on setting payouts and not a forecast.

Where it breaks (seen in our runs)

  • Collinearity: SCB + drawdown together gave unstable, sign-flipping coefficients (Spearman 0.92 between them).
  • Small yearly cross-sections (18–35 banks): the package's sandwich p-values are optimistic; we used resampling instead.
  • Ties: many zero buybacks; handled as ½, but they dilute the buyback leg.
  • Timing: payouts in calendar \(Y\) partly precede year-\(Y\) results; next-year checks were weaker (next-year dividend share vs drawdown: PI 0.46 per IQR, p = 0.17).
  • Not available: firm-level baseline projections, so "versus baseline" cannot be asked.

What we'd do next

Top pick: carry the PIM to Q2 as a text-versus-numbers check, "within a subsegment, how often does a program labelled grow actually grow faster than its peers?", and require it to beat the 0.78 AUC from the 11-13 peer comparison. In parallel, the Math card would give a proper firm-clustered variance so we stop relying on the bootstrap.

Benchmark against ARDL and SARIMAX (added 2026-11-17, at Jared's request)

Jared asked for ARDL and SARIMAX benchmarks beside OLS, and said to start the Engineering and Math avenues only if the benchmark "looks promising". We wrote down what "promising" means before running anything (PREREG.md, section A).

  • Design. For each test year 2016–2025, every model is fitted only on earlier years, then asked to order the banks tested that year. The common yardstick is the share of same-year bank pairs put in the right order by payout (0.5 = coin flip). This is a model-comparison check, not a forecast; no bank-level predictions are published.
  • Models. OLS and PIM on the two stress metrics (drawdown and pre-tax income); a pooled ARDL(1,0) adding last year's payout; a per-firm SARIMAX(1,0,0) with the stress metrics as exogenous inputs (annual data, so no seasonal part; needs 5+ years of the firm's own history, which leaves 172 of 281 test rows); and PIM given the same information as ARDL.

How to read the benchmark
  1. Last year's payout does most of the work: adding it lifts every model from about 0.67 to about 0.78 on payout/RWA. Payout rankings are sticky.
  2. Given the same inputs, PIM and ARDL are level on payout/RWA (+0.004; 90% firm-bootstrap interval −0.003 to +0.013).
  3. On the unstable payout/NI ratio, PIM beats ARDL clearly (+0.068; +0.030 to +0.101), the same robustness the 11-16 trimming check showed.
  4. Against per-firm SARIMAX on payout/NI, PIM is 0.020 behind (−0.095 to +0.042): inside the noise, but our pre-set rule needed PIM at or above SARIMAX there.
What it means for a decision-maker

On the ordinary payout ratio, PIM does as well as the standard time-series models. On the noisy payout-to-income ratio it does better than ARDL and about as well as SARIMAX. By the bar we set in advance, that is "close but not promising", so we have not started the Rust fitter or the clustered-variance math. Whether "close" is good enough is Jared's call.

Same trimming test, applied to ARDL (descriptive). Dropping the 8 most extreme payout/NI ratios cuts ARDL's long-run drawdown effect from −0.078 to −0.043 (−44%), about the same as OLS (−43%); the PIM effect moved under 2% on 11-16.

Where the benchmark breaks. Per-firm SARIMAX has at most 12 annual points per bank and skips untested years, so it covers only 172 test rows and is noisy. Ten of the 40 PIM fits ended on nleqslv's step-size tolerance rather than its function tolerance (reported, not hidden).
