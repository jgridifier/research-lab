The statistic

$$D(c,r) = \sum_{s=1}^{n} \frac{B_s(c,r)}{k_s(c)}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sum_r D(c,r) = n \ \text{ for every column } c .$$

Each series spreads exactly one unit of weight over the pixels it touches in each column. The naive alternative (the "line count" heatmap) is \(\sum_s B_s(c,r)\), where a steep line contributes \(k_s(c)\) units.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · the problem: ink grows with slopeOver a time step \(\Delta x\), a segment with slope \(f'\) has length \(\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2} = \Delta x\sqrt{1+f'^2}\). Uniform drawing deposits ink proportional to length, so steep (often noisy) series are over-represented (paper §3.1).
Step 2 · divide by arc lengthWeighting each segment by \(1/\sqrt{1+f'^2}\) gives every series the same weight per unit of time, whatever its slope.
Step 3 · the pixel shortcutIn one pixel column the arc length is proportional to the number of pixels the line lights, \(k_s(c)\) (one segment per column, paper §3.1.1). Dividing by \(k_s(c)\) is the discrete version of Step 2. A flat line has \(k=1\) and is unchanged.
Step 4 · the column is now a distributionEach column sums to \(n\), so \(D(c,\cdot)/n\) is an empirical density of "where the series are" at time \(c\): ridges are common paths, faint strands are outliers. The method has no parameters beyond canvas size; colour scale and display blur are presentation (paper §3.2).
Step 5 · our fix for start/stop seriesIf series \(s\) does not cover column \(c\), \(k_s(c) = 0\) and the reference computes \(0/0 =\) NaN, which poisons the column sum. Gap-safe rule: \(B_s/k_s := 0\) when \(k_s(c)=0\). A live-count strip \(n(c) = \#\{s: k_s(c)>0\}\) is drawn alongside so an entry wave is not misread as a density change.

Inference

DenseLines is a picture, not a test. Anything read off it (like the residual "brush" that selects programs ending above a valley) needs its own null; the valley's existence depended on smoothing (2–4 px yes, 6–8 px no), which is why the New-use card asks for a bandwidth-free dip test with a year-shuffle null.

Toy example

Two series on a 3-column canvas. Series A is flat: it lights 1 pixel in each column. Series B is flat in columns 1 and 3 but jumps in column 2, lighting 3 pixels there.

Line count: column 2 totals \(1 + 3 = 4\) units, so B's jump looks like three series. DenseLines: B's three pixels get \(1/3\) each; every column totals \(1+1 = 2 = n\). If B stopped after column 2, the reference would compute \(0/0\) in column 3 and blank it; gap-safe gives column 3 a total of 1 and the live-count strip shows 1.

How it differs from the standard tool. A spaghetti plot draws every line on top of the others: with 1,052 lines the overlap hides where most programs are. A plain 2-D histogram of the points ignores the lines between years. A naive line heatmap over-weights steep series. DenseLines is the line heatmap with each series normalised per column, so it shows where the series are, one vote each.
