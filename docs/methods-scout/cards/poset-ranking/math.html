Assumptions

  • Each indicator has an agreed direction (more drawdown, lower pre-tax income, more losses = worse). Nothing is assumed about their relative importance or scale.
  • Dominance is read exactly: a 0.1 pp difference counts as much as 5 pp. Measurement noise in the projections is ignored (the Math avenue fixes this).
  • For rank probabilities, all linear extensions are treated as equally likely (the "uniform over consistent rankings" convention of Brüggemann and of Schoch 2018). It is a reference measure, not a model of how the Fed or a bank weighs indicators.
  • For the tests: under the null, payouts are exchangeable across firms within a test year.

The estimator and the statistic

Rank probabilities and expected rank over all rankings that respect dominance:

$$P(\mathrm{rk}(u)=k)=\frac{\big|\{\ell\in\mathcal L(\preceq):\ell(u)=k\}\big|}{|\mathcal L(\preceq)|},\qquad \rho(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\,P(\mathrm{rk}(u)=k).$$

They are computed exactly on the lattice of ideals. With \(e(D)\) the number of linear extensions of the sub-poset \(D\) (ways to build \(D\) from the bottom) and \(\bar e(D)\) the number of ways to finish from \(D\) to \(V\):

$$|\{\ell:\ell(u)=k\}|=\sum_{\substack{D\in\mathcal I,\ |D|=k-1\\ u\notin D,\ D\cup\{u\}\in\mathcal I}} e(D)\;\bar e(D\cup\{u\}).$$

The null and multiple testing

  • Null: within each test year, payouts are exchangeable across firms (the dominance structure is kept fixed). 4,999 shuffles for \(C_{\mathrm{dom}}\) pooled over years (one-sided); 1,999 for the trade-off agreements (two-sided about 0.5); 999 for the SCB-vs-path comparison.
  • Holm over the five orderings in the trade-off analysis (equal-weight, in-sample OLS, and the three single indicators).
  • Same firm pairs recur across years; the within-year shuffle is valid for "no association in any year" but the pooled p-values are not firm-clustered. Year-to-year stability is reported descriptively.
Toy example (computed by cards/_toys/toys.py)

Four firms, two indicators (higher = worse): A = (1, 1), B = (2, 3), C = (3, 2), D = (4, 4). Dominance pairs: A≺B, A≺C, A≺D, B≺D, C≺D; B and C are a trade-off (B worse on indicator 2, C on indicator 1). The ideals are ∅, {A}, {A,B}, {A,C}, {A,B,C}, {A,B,C,D} (6), and there are 2 linear extensions, ABCD and ACBD. Expected ranks: A 1, B 2.5, C 2.5, D 4; P(rank of B = 2) = 0.5. With payouts A 3.0, B 1.0, C 2.0, D 0.5, dominance concordance is 1.0 (5 of 5). On the trade-off pair, indicator 2 agrees (B is worse and pays less) and indicator 1 does not (C is worse but pays more): exactly the pattern that sets pre-tax income against loan losses on our data.

How it differs from the standard tool. The usual approach builds one composite (equal weights, PC1, or a regression) and correlates its ranks with payouts. That assumes the indicators are substitutable at fixed rates and turns every trade-off into an order the analyst chose. The partial order assumes only the direction of each indicator. It keeps "not comparable" as an answer, prices the ambiguity (rank intervals and rank probabilities), and splits the evidence into a weight-free part (dominance pairs) and a part where we can ask which indicator payouts follow. A composite can report a weaker average correlation; it cannot show that two of its inputs pull in opposite directions.
