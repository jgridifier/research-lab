(a) Robust at homepromising but thin

What the home field has established

  • Taylor’s law is long established. Variance grows as a power of the mean, \(\sigma^2 = a\mu^z\). It has been reported since Taylor (1961) across insects, fish and plants. Cohen & Xu (PNAS 112:7749–7754, 2015; abstract read) show that random block sampling from any skewed distribution with four finite moments produces it. They give formulae for the slope and its standard error. A follow-up exchange (Chen; Cohen & Xu’s reply, doi:10.1073/pnas.1507551112) disputed when samples do not support it.
  • The mean-variance portfolio effect is newer and thinner. It rests on one methods paper. From its summary: the widely used average-CV portfolio effect implicitly assumes z = 2. Across 51 metapopulations and 1,070 subpopulations, the average-CV effect was stronger than the mean-variance one in 84% of metapopulations, by up to sevenfold. The gap was largest for reef fishes and moths (low z); the two agreed for salmon (z ≈ 2). The paper has 88 citations (DOI metadata). Tilman, Lehman & Bristow (1998) is cited in the paper as the theoretical basis; we did not read it.
  • Package. v0.1.0, single author, no test suite, last push 2025-06. Today’s reproduction of the package vignette matched exactly: pink salmon pinkbr gives PEmv = 1.412 (vignette 1.412), PEavg-CV = 1.517 (vignette 1.517) and z = 1.98.
  • Known failure modes. The single-asset variance is extrapolated to the portfolio’s size. The package’s CI covers only the Taylor-fit uncertainty, not the portfolio CV. Today the quadratic variant blew up (Corning mock: 34.7, vs 0.83 linear and 0.86 robust) over a 2.1-decade extrapolation.

Verdict: promising but thin. The scaling law is solid. The portfolio-effect metric has one primary paper and an unmaintained, untested package, though the package is simple enough to verify by hand (we did, to 1e-15).

(b) What it finds here

Mapping the home-field set-up to ours

  • Who plays what. Each stream’s salmon run becomes a program’s annual actual spend. The metapopulation becomes the firm (or a segment). Taylor’s z becomes how a program’s volatility scales with its size.
  • Assumptions that break. Salmon series run for decades; ours have 5–6 points, so each program’s variance has 3–4 degrees of freedom after detrending. Budgets trend, so we use the package’s linear_detrended variant. Programs enter and exit, but the package needs a complete matrix. The mocks are raked to public totals, which constrains the firm total. Abundance noise is sampling noise; budget noise is accounting and planning noise.
  • What the standard toolkit gets wrong. The finance view is the diversification ratio \(\sum_i\sigma_i/\sigma_p\) (3.14 Corning mock, 3.10 MS mock), or its ecology twin, the average-CV PE (3.36 / 3.25). Both say the total is about three times steadier than its parts. That reading assumes every program’s volatility scales one-for-one with size (z = 2). The fitted z is 1.67 / 1.77, so big programs are proportionally calmer. Measured against a single program of the same size, the portfolio is no steadier: PEmv 0.99 / 1.15. On the mocks’ noise-free true levels the gap persists (Corning mock: average-CV 1.54 vs mean-variance 0.25).
  • Carries further? Yes, to anything with many units of very different size: branch networks, product lines, fund families by AUM, loan books by obligor.

What it is. Fit Taylor’s law across programs, \(\log v_i = \log a + z \log m_i\), where \(m_i\) is program \(i\)’s mean spend and \(v_i\) the variance of its linearly detrended spend. Extrapolate it to the portfolio mean \(M = \mathbb{E}[\sum_i x_{it}]\) to get the CV a single program of that size would have. Compare that with the portfolio’s observed CV: $$\mathrm{PE}_{mv} = \frac{\sqrt{a M^{z}}/M}{\mathrm{CV}_{\text{portfolio}}},\qquad \mathrm{PE}_{avgCV} = \frac{\tfrac1n\sum_i \mathrm{CV}_i}{\mathrm{CV}_{\text{portfolio}}}.$$ With z = 2 the single-asset CV does not depend on size, and the two measures coincide. With z < 2 a large program is calmer, so the average CV of small programs overstates what an equally large undivided program would show. PE > 1 means the portfolio is steadier than one equal-sized program. Why it is fast: it is one log-log regression and one prediction. Our vectorised version takes 0.1–0.15 ms per portfolio.

Year-shuffle null. Shuffling each program’s years keeps its mean and variance but breaks co-movement. The observed portfolio PE is 0.99 against a null median of 7.28 (5–95%: 4.27–15.88) on the Corning mock, and 1.15 against 13.22 (7.27–34.92) on the MS mock; both p = 0.001. Group screen: an honest miss. As a detector of hidden co-moving groups, the plain standard (mean pairwise YoY correlation against random same-size sets) puts every planted cluster at the 100th percentile. The mean-variance PE puts them anywhere from the 0th to the 81st percentile. It also produces extreme values for small groups (up to 283 for a 12-program group) because the within-group Taylor fit is poor. Trade-off clusters do sit a little high (71st and 81st percentiles), as compensation would predict, but not significantly. Use correlation (or the 11-13 residual DenseLines) to find groups. Use PE to say how much a group’s size, rather than diversification, explains its steadiness.

Per-program flag (Q2.3). We also flagged programs by their residual from the Taylor line (size-aware volatility) and compared that with the CV. Neither found the planted “movers” (cluster members with large loading × factor range). AUC: Taylor residual 0.34 / 0.30, CV 0.32 / 0.28, raw variance 0.50 / 0.47 (Corning / MS mock). The Taylor residual does remove the size bias. Its Spearman with log size is 0.02 / −0.07, against −0.24 / −0.28 for CV. CV’s top 50 are small programs (mean $3.4M vs $5.0M overall on Corning; $11.0M vs $26.8M on MS). On the MS mock both track the true-level volatility ranking about equally (Spearman 0.62 vs 0.60); on the Corning mock neither does (−0.04, 0.15). Five or six points per program are not enough to rank programs by volatility. Pool (ebnm, 11-13) before ranking.

Timing. The 999-shuffle null takes 5.5 s (Corning mock) and 10.7 s (MS mock) with the vectorised PE. Through the package call it would be about 999 × 0.41 s ≈ 7 min on the MS mock; our first attempt did not finish one firm in 16 minutes. The whole script runs in 63 s.

Limitations and verdict

Limitations. SYNTHETIC data only. The mocks rake programs to public totals, which builds in synchrony. Balanced panels drop 24–28% of programs. Short series make per-program variances noisy (the Taylor fit pools them, which is why it still works). The mean-variance PE hinges on a two-decade extrapolation and on the variant chosen. The package CI ignores portfolio-CV uncertainty.

Verdict: interesting. It is a one-line correction that changes the headline answer to “is the book diversified?”. Its picture (the Taylor plot with the z = 2 line) is clear. It is not a structure-discovery tool on short series. It needs the noise correction (top pick) before any real-data use.

(a) Robust at homewell established

What the home field has established

  • Origin. A semiparametric model for \(P(Y \le Y^* \mid X, X^*)\), with asymptotic normality of the estimator, a consistent sandwich covariance, and a simulation study (JRSS-B 2012 abstract). The DOI metadata lists 163 citations.
  • Follow-ups. Amorim et al. (2018; abstract and introduction read) describe PIMs as generating classical rank tests (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, Friedman), citing De Neve & Thas (2015), which we did not read. They show that asymptotic inference is poor in small samples and that β has a large finite-sample bias. Their bias-reduced bootstrap and adjusted jackknife empirical likelihood fix it.
  • Implementation. pim 2.0.4 on CRAN (2025-01), testthat in Suggests, maintained by the Ghent group; the original interface lives on as pimold on R-Forge. Today’s reproduction: on the package’s FEVData, a PIM with one binary covariate gives PI = 0.749275, identical to the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney probabilistic index (0.749275). That is the special case the theory says it should reproduce.
  • Known failure modes. Small samples (ours: 18–35 firms per year). Dependent pseudo-observations. Ties (many zero buybacks). Collinear covariates give unstable, sign-flipping coefficients (seen today with SCB + drawdown).

Verdict: well established as a statistical framework with theory, simulations and a maintained package. It is little used outside biostatistics and nearly unknown in finance.

(b) What it finds here

Mapping the home-field set-up to ours

  • Who plays what. Patients become banks. Treatment arms or doses become stress metrics (SA drawdown, SA pre-tax income, SCB, the Adverse–SA gap). The outcome becomes payout/RWA, or its dividend and buyback legs. Pairs are formed only within a test year (pim’s custom compare matrix), so year-to-year shifts in the scenario and in payout norms cancel out.
  • Assumptions that break. Firms recur across 13 years, so pairs are not independent across years; we use a firm-block bootstrap (299) and a within-year permutation null (499). Each year’s cross-section is small. Payout ratios have mass at zero and extreme values when net income is near zero.
  • What the standard toolkit gets wrong. OLS with year fixed effects on payout/net income ~ drawdown gives −0.070. Trimming the 8 most extreme ratios (2% of 360) cuts that to −0.040, a 43% drop. The PIM effect moves from 0.4675 to 0.4697. On the dividend share before 2020, OLS sees nothing (t = −0.94); PIM gives p = 0.014 (Holm 0.13, so suggestive only).
  • Carries further? Yes, to any small, heavy-tailed cross-section compared within blocks: Q2 programs within subsegments (the top-pick avenue), funds within strategy, dealers within week.

What it is. For two banks \(i, j\) in the same test year, $$P(Y_i \preccurlyeq Y_j \mid X_i, X_j) = \operatorname{expit}\big((X_j - X_i)^\top\beta\big),\qquad Y_i \preccurlyeq Y_j \iff Y_i < Y_j \text{ or, for ties, half.}$$ \(\operatorname{expit}(\beta\,\Delta)\) is the probability that the bank higher by \(\Delta\) on the stress metric has the higher payout. 0.5 means no ordering. It is estimated from the \(\binom{n}{2}\) within-year pseudo-observations by estimating equations, with a sandwich variance. Why this, not OLS or IDR: it reads ranks, so a near-zero net income cannot dominate. It is a regression, so it gives a partial effect: drawdown with pre-tax income held fixed. The 11-14 IDR gave monotone conditional distributions, not a partial effect size. Its effect size is a single, plain probability. Fast: 49,500 pairs fit in 0.05 s; all 29 models with 499 permutations and 299 bootstraps each ran in about 3 minutes.

Reading. Before 2020, banks with deeper stress drawdowns paid lower dividends relative to RWA than their same-year peers (0.36 per IQR, Holm p = 0.02). Buybacks showed no ordering (0.49, p = 0.82), and the dividend share of gross distributions was lower (0.43, p = 0.014, Holm 0.13). After 2020, when capital actions in the projections became standardised and the SCB arrived, buybacks pick up the ordering (0.44, p = 0.034) and the dividend-share effect disappears (0.54, p = 0.30). Only the pre-2020 dividend result survives Holm. So the answer to Q3.5 is tentative: before 2020 the stress ranking showed up in the payout mix; after 2020 it may have moved into the buyback leg. The SCB itself orders buybacks weakly (0.45, p = 0.056) and dividends not at all (p = 0.33).

Limitations and verdict

Limitations. Descriptive association only; the work makes no claim about how a bank should set its payouts. Payouts in calendar Y partly precede year-Y results, which is why the next-year check is reported. Small yearly cross-sections (18–35) are the case where Amorim et al. found asymptotic inference unreliable, so we lean on permutation and firm-block bootstrap rather than the sandwich p. 29 models in three families; Holm is applied within each family. Firm-level baseline projections are not published, so “versus baseline” cannot be asked. Y-9C overlaps the held-out window of the pre-registered trading study; nothing here forecasts.

Verdict: recommend for promote workflow (Jared decides; nothing is promoted). It is well grounded at home and robust where OLS is fragile. It gives effect sizes a reader can say out loud, and it answered two Q3 sub-questions in one pass. The top-pick avenue carries it to Q2.2.
