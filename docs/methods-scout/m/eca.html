Assumptions

  • Events are well defined: here "top (or bottom) 10% of weekly changes", fixed before the run.
  • For the analytic (Poisson) p-value: both series are independent, sparse, homogeneous Poisson processes. For the circular-shift p-value we use instead: each series keeps its own timing and clustering; only the alignment between them is random.
  • Stationary enough that a shift of the whole series is a fair "no-link" world (no strong trend in event frequency).

The statistic

$$r_p(\Delta T,\tau)=\frac1{N_A}\sum_{i=1}^{N_A}\Theta\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{N_B}\mathbf 1_{[0,\Delta T]}\big((t^A_i-\tau)-t^B_j\big)\Big],\qquad r_t(\Delta T,\tau)=\frac1{N_B}\sum_{j=1}^{N_B}\Theta\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N_A}\mathbf 1_{[0,\Delta T]}\big((t^A_i-\tau)-t^B_j\big)\Big]$$

\(\Theta\) is 1 if its argument is positive, so several \(B\) events in one window count once. \(r_p\) asks "what share of \(A\) events had a \(B\) event shortly before?"; \(r_t\) asks "what share of \(B\) events were followed by an \(A\) event?". Directionality comes from comparing \(r_p(A\mid B)\) with the same rate with the roles swapped.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · chance coincidence under independenceThe window before one \(A\) event covers \(\Delta T+1\) of the \(T-\tau\) usable steps. If the \(N_B\) events land independently and uniformly, the chance that none falls in it is \(q=\big(1-\tfrac{\Delta T+1}{T-\tau}\big)^{N_B}\), so each \(A\) event coincides with probability \(1-q\).
Step 2 · the analytic p-valueTreating the \(N_A\) windows as independent trials, the number of coincidences \(K_p=N_A r_p\) is Binomial\((N_A,1-q)\), so \(p=\sum_{K\ge K_p}\binom{N_A}{K}(1-q)^K q^{N_A-K}\). With our numbers (\(T=399\), \(N_B=40\), \(\Delta T=1\), \(\tau=0\)): \(1-q = 1-(1-2/399)^{40}\approx 0.18\) (computed), the "random placement" level on the chart.
Step 3 · where step 2 breaksFails spikes cluster (a spike week is followed by another 9% of the time; DVP surges 14%; positioning moves 18%), so windows are not independent trials and the binomial variance is too small. That is why we judge significance with circular shifts of \(B\) (4,999 per test, shift ≥ 8 weeks), which keep each series' clustering, and why the Poisson test over-rejects on our data (finding 3).

The null and multiple testing

  • Main family F1: 8 ordered pairs × 3 lags × 2 rates = 48 tests, circular-shift p-values, Holm over all 48 (pre-registered).
  • Exact-lag follow-up (\(\Delta T=0\), \(\tau=0,1,2\)) and the reverse direction: added after F1, reported as follow-up, not familywise-controlled.
  • Calibration check: false-positive rate of the Poisson test on 2,000 shifted pairs (pre-registered flag at 10%).
Toy example (computed by cards/_toys/toys.py)

Ten weeks. \(A\) events in weeks 3 and 7; \(B\) events in weeks 2, 6 and 9; \(\Delta T=1\), \(\tau=0\). Week 3 has a \(B\) in weeks 2–3 (yes, week 2); week 7 has one in 6–7 (yes). So \(K_p=2\), \(r_p=1.0\). Of the \(B\) events, weeks 2 and 6 are followed by an \(A\) within a week, week 9 is not: \(K_t=2\), \(r_t=0.667\). Chance that a two-week window misses all three \(B\) events: \(q=(1-2/10)^3=0.512\). Poisson p for \(r_p\): \((1-0.512)^2=0.238\); for \(r_t\): 0.295. With so few events nothing is significant.

How it differs from the standard tool. Cross-correlation and Granger causality use every week and every size of move, assume a linear and symmetric link (a big build and a big unwind count equally, with opposite signs), and are dominated by ordinary weeks. ECA throws away the middle of the distribution on purpose and asks only about the tails, separately for each tail and each direction. On our data the cross-correlation at lag 1 is r = −0.16: it is "significant" but says nothing about which tail carries it. ECA shows that unwinds carry it and builds do not.

Followed: Donges, Schleussner, Siegmund & Donner (2016), EPJ Special Topics 225:471–487 (arXiv:1508.03534; definitions, eqs. 3–4 of the arXiv version, read); Siegmund, Siegmund & Donges (2016, arXiv), CoinCalc, arXiv:1603.05038 (eqs. 1–2 and the Poisson test, read; both worked examples reproduced to the printed digits). Finding 1

Fails spikes follow big leveraged-fund unwinds by one week.

Of 39 financing-fails spike weeks (top 10% of weekly log change, 2018-05 to 2025-12), 26% come exactly one week after a big unwind week (bottom 10% change in leveraged-fund net short, UST 2Y+5Y+10Y futures). Random placement gives about 10% (circular-shift p = 0.008). Same or previous week: 38% vs 18%, Holm p = 0.0096 over 48 pre-registered tests.

Big build weeks: 10% one week before (p = 0.54), no different from chance. Reverse direction (unwind one week after a fails spike): 15%, p = 0.16.

The same/previous-week precursor rate beats its null 95th percentile in 2018-05..2022-06 (42% vs 37%) and 2022-07..2025-12 (35% vs 30%), and at 80% (55% vs 44%) and 95% (25% vs 20%) event thresholds: all five pre-registered checks pass.

Finding 3

The textbook Poisson test is too generous on these clustered series.

On 2,000 circularly shifted (independent by construction) pairs, CoinCalc's Poisson test rejects at 5% in 9.2% (fails ← spread) and 9.4% (DVP ← fails) of cases. Overall, 15 of 48 tests have Poisson p < 0.05; only 10 pass the circular-shift null and 3 survive Holm.

Below our pre-set 'miscalibrated' flag of 10%, but nearly double the nominal 5%. That motivates the Math avenue.

Finding 4

Monthly Form PF borrowing spikes do not line up with fails.

Form PF tab 8.27 prime-broker and total borrowing growth (top 20% months, 2013-05..2025-12, 152 months) shows no coincidence with fails-spike months at lags 0, 3 or 6 months, in either direction (smallest p = 0.24; all Holm p = 1).

Consistent with the 11-14 CRQA (prime-broker growth: no link). Low power: with 3-month windows and 31 events, the null 95th percentile is already about 0.6.

What it means for a decision-maker

When hedge funds and other leveraged traders cut their Treasury-futures short positions sharply in a week, a jump in settlement fails at the primary dealers tends to follow the next week, about 2.5 times as often as chance would give. When they add to those shorts, nothing like that happens. A normal correlation sees only a weak link and cannot tell the two cases apart. This is a pattern in public aggregates, not proof that the unwinds cause the fails (both may answer the same market shock), and not a trading signal.

Where it breaks (seen in our runs)

  • Common shocks. March 2020 contributes three of the 15 coincident weeks; a shared market shock could drive both legs. The conditional ECA (top pick) tests this.
  • Few events. 39–44 events per series; the trigger version fails in the second half.
  • Timing of the positioning data. TFF positions are as of Tuesday; fails are weekly to Wednesday. A "same week" coincidence can mix one day of overlap with the prior six.
  • Reference implementation. In CoinCalc 2.0, CC.eca.ts with sigtest = "shuffle" or "surrogate" stopped with "object 'Pprec' not found" on our data; the waiting-time surrogate ran (1,000 reps in 30.0 s; our circular-shift version 0.08 s, a different null).
  • Monthly Form PF has too few months for this method at a 3-month window (finding 4).

What we'd do next

Promising but thin

What the home field has established

  • Origin. Donges et al. (2016, EPJ Special Topics 225:471–487; arXiv:1508.03534, definitions read) define precursor and trigger coincidence rates with lag and tolerance, and tests based on Poisson processes or on point processes with a given inter-event-time distribution. Their application: floods as triggers of epidemic outbreaks, country-level, since the 1950s (abstract read).
  • Package. CoinCalc (Siegmund, Siegmund & Donges, arXiv:1603.05038, 2016, read): R implementation with Poisson, shuffle and surrogate tests and two worked geoscience examples. We reproduced both to the printed digits. GitHub repository: 19 stars, last push 2017-01-25 (GitHub API, read 2026-11-17). The PIK pyunicorn library (231 stars, last push 2026-11-12) also carries ECA in its event-series module (tutorial read).
  • Follow-ups. Odenweller & Donner (2020, PRE 101:052213; abstract and conclusions read) compare ECA with event synchronization in coupled autoregressive simulations. Event synchronization is biased by clustered, serially dependent events; ECA is "significantly less susceptible" and recommended for time-resolved work. Schleussner et al. (2016, PNAS 113:9216; abstract read) use ECA for climate disasters and armed-conflict outbreaks.
  • Known failure modes. The Poisson test assumes independent sparse events (we measured a 9.2–9.4% false-positive rate at nominal 5% on clustered series); results depend on the event threshold and window, which must be fixed in advance; shared drivers are not separated without the conditional variant.

Verdict: promising but thin. The statistic is simple and widely applied in climate-impact work, and one simulation study backs it. But most validation is by the originating PIK group, the reference R package has not been updated since 2017, and two of its significance options errored on our data.

1 · Engineering · Effort S
A fast ECA engine with clustering-aware nulls

A vectorised NumPy core (Rust later if the grid grows) that runs every ordered pair × lag × window across all Q1 series, with circular-shift, block-shuffle and waiting-time surrogates. CoinCalc 2.0 is the oracle; we already have a NumPy port at parity.

Math sketch. Store each series as a sorted event list and a prefix sum \(c_B(t)=\sum_{s\le t}B_s\); a window \([t-\tau-\Delta T,\,t-\tau]\) has a coincidence iff \(c_B(t-\tau)-c_B(t-\tau-\Delta T-1)>0\). A circular shift by \(s\) is an index offset, so all \(R\) surrogates are one \((R\times N_A)\) gather instead of \(R\) loops.

Why it matters here. Q1 has about a dozen candidate weekly series (venues, tenors, fail types, positioning by contract); a full pre-registered grid is several thousand tests, each needing thousands of surrogates.

Closest prior work: CoinCalc (Siegmund et al., arXiv 2016, arXiv:1603.05038) and pyunicorn's event-series module (pik-copan/pyunicorn); neither offers circular-shift nulls in CoinCalc's ts mode.

Test (pass bar set now). Parity ≤ 1e-12 with CoinCalc on both paper examples and our 48 tests (rates and Poisson p); waiting-time surrogate p within Monte-Carlo error of CoinCalc's; the full 12-series grid (132 pairs × 6 lags × 2 rates, 4,999 surrogates) in under 10 s on the box.

2 · Math · Effort M
An analytic null for clustered events

Replace the Poisson null (independent, sparse events) with one where each series is a two-state Markov chain fitted to its own event clustering, so the p-value is cheap and calibrated without surrogates.

Math sketch. For \(B\) a stationary 0/1 Markov chain with event rate \(\pi\) and stay probability \(p_{00}\) in the no-event state, \(P(\text{no }B\text{ in a window of length }w)=(1-\pi)\,p_{00}^{\,w-1}\), replacing \((1-w/T)^{N_B}\). Then \(K_p\) is a sum of dependent indicators; with \(A\) also Markov, use the effective number of independent \(A\) clusters \(N_A^{\mathrm{eff}}=N_A(1-p^{A}_{11})\) in a binomial, or a Markov-modulated Poisson–binomial for an exact tail.

Why it matters here. Our Poisson false-positive rate is 9.2–9.4% at nominal 5%, and every weekly funding series we have clusters.

Closest prior work: Donges et al. (2016) mention tests for point processes with a prescribed inter-event-time distribution (abstract read); Odenweller & Donner (2020, PRE 101:052213) correct normalisation and boundary effects. No closed-form Markov null found.

Test (pass bar set now). On 2,000 circularly shifted pairs per Q1 pair, false-positive rate within 4–6% at nominal 5% (Poisson: 9.2%); on planted couplings in simulated Markov event series, power at least that of the 4,999-shift test.

3 · New use on our data · Effort S · ★ Top pick
Conditional ECA: unwinds or a common shock? (Q1.2, Q1.5)

CoinCalc already ships a conditional variant (CC.ceca.ts). Ask whether "fails spike one week after an unwind" survives when weeks with a VIX spike (FRED VIXCLS, on the box) or a DVP-spread spike are held out, and whether the unwind–fails link is in the 2Y or the 10Y contract.

Math sketch. \(r_p(A\mid B,\neg C)=\tfrac{1}{N_A^{\neg C}}\sum_{i:\,C\text{ absent near }t_i}\Theta[\cdot]\): the precursor rate computed only on \(A\) events with no \(C\) event in \([t_i-\tau-\Delta T,\,t_i]\). The null shifts \(B\) circularly with \(C\) and \(A\) fixed.

Why it matters here. It is the cheapest way to tell "leveraged funds unwinding creates fails" from "a volatility shock causes both", the core of Q1.2 and Q1.5.

Closest prior work: conditional coincidence in CoinCalc 2.0 (CC.ceca.ts, function signature read on the box; no paper read for it).

Test (pass bar set now). Pre-registered: lag 1, \(\Delta T=0\), conditioning on VIX top-10% weekly changes and on DVP-spread top-10% changes separately; the conditional precursor rate must exceed its circular-shift null 95th percentile in both, with Holm over the two.

Q1.1 · Lead/lag

At the extremes, positioning leads funding stress: a fails spike follows a big leveraged-fund unwind one week later 26% of the time vs about 10% by chance (p = 0.008); the reverse is not significant. Monthly Form PF borrowing spikes show no coincidence with fails at 0–6 months.

Q1.2 · Co-movement vs coincidence in stress

Three of the 15 coincident weeks are in March 2020, and three are in 2025 (Feb 19, Mar 5, Sep 24). The rolling 104-week rate is above the chance level in 73 of 74 windows (the exception ends 2022-03-09), so the link is not only a 2020 artefact; the rolling rate is descriptive, not tested window by window. Whether a shared shock drives both is the top pick.

Q1.5 · Positioning vs funding

The 11-15 CCM found no positioning ↔ fails link while linear Granger rejected both ways. ECA localises it: the link lives in the unwind tail and one direction only. Builds show nothing.
