Assumptions

  • Few time points, many units (the method enumerates shapes; it is meant for \(T\le 8\)).
  • Only the shape matters: assignment by correlation ignores the size of a move, so a nearly flat noisy program still gets a shape.
  • Under "no real shape", the time points of each unit are exchangeable: any reordering of a program's years is equally likely. A shared trend (inflation, raking to totals) breaks this and will make trending shapes significant.
  • Units are independent for the binomial count test.

The estimator and the statistic

Model shapes start at 0 and move by an integer step in \(\{-c,\dots,c\}\) at each time point. A greedy max–min rule keeps \(m\) shapes that are as different as possible (the exact max–min set is NP-hard, proved in the paper's supplement). Each unit goes to its most correlated shape, ties split equally:

$$j^*(g)=\arg\max_{j\in\mathcal M}\rho(v_g,\,p_j),\qquad n_j=\sum_g \frac{\mathbf 1[j\in J^*(g)]}{|J^*(g)|}.$$

The expected count averages the same assignment over all reorderings \(\pi\) of the time points (\(T!\) of them: 720 or 120), each re-anchored to start at 0:

$$e_j=\frac1{T!}\sum_{\pi}\sum_g \frac{\mathbf 1[j\in J^*(g;\pi)]}{|J^*(g;\pi)|},\qquad p_j=P\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,\,e_j/N)\ge n_j\big).$$

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · shapes are chosen before seeing dataBecause \(\mathcal M\) depends only on \(T\) and \(c\), the 50 shapes are the same for the real data, the shuffled data and the noise-free data (we checked: identical profile tables). So counts are comparable across runs and the test is not "the cluster centre was fitted to the data, then found significant".
Step 2 · what a shuffle does to one unitReorder unit \(g\)'s values by \(\pi\) and re-anchor: \(v_g^{\pi}=x_{g\pi}-x_{g\pi(0)}\). Correlation is unchanged by adding a constant, so \(\rho(v_g^\pi,p_j)=\rho(x_{g\pi},p_j)\): the anchoring step does not matter for assignment, only the order does. Averaging over all \(\pi\) gives each unit's chance of landing on shape \(j\) when its own values are kept but their time order carries no information.
Step 3 · from per-unit chances to a count testIf units are independent and each lands on \(j\) with probability \(q_{gj}\), then \(n_j\) is Poisson-binomial with mean \(\sum_g q_{gj}=e_j\). STEM approximates it by \(\mathrm{Bin}(N,e_j/N)\), which has the same mean and a variance \(\ge\) the Poisson-binomial's, so the tail is, if anything, conservative. Bonferroni over the \(m\) shapes gives the 5% familywise level used here (\(p_j<0.001\)).
Step 4 · mergingSignificant shapes whose shapes correlate at \(\ge 0.7\) are grouped greedily into clusters (same colour in the grid), the cluster built around the shape that gathers most units.

The null and multiple testing

  • Null: time points exchangeable within each unit (all \(T!\) orderings, including the first point, the STEM default).
  • Bonferroni over the 50 shapes (STEM default; FDR is an option we did not use).
  • Calibration seen: in 20 pure-noise simulations of the paper's design, 2 runs had one false shape (10% familywise); on our shuffled mocks, 1 and 0 shapes.
Toy example (computed by cards/_toys/toys.py)

Four programs over three years (log levels): (10, 10.2, 10.5), (8, 8.3, 8.5), (9, 8.7, 9.1), (7, 7.4, 7.2). Two shapes: up (0, 1, 2) and dip (0, −1, 0). Programs 1, 2 and 4 correlate best with "up", program 3 with "dip": counts (3, 1). Averaging the assignment over the 6 orderings of the three years gives expected counts (1.833, 2.167). Binomial tail \(P(\mathrm{Bin}(4,1.833/4)\ge 3)=0.253\) for "up" and 0.956 for "dip". With four programs nothing is significant; with 778 the same arithmetic gives 69 vs 14 for the top shape.

How it differs from the standard tool. K-means (or hierarchical clustering) fits centres to the data and always returns \(k\) clusters; it is blind to time order (shuffling the columns of every unit the same way changes nothing), and it has no notion of "more units than chance". STEM fixes the shapes in advance, uses time order through the shuffle null, and reports only shapes that are over-full. The price: it ignores move size, and with few, noisy points a group must be large to show (finding 3).

What we found

Finding 1

It tells real shapes from noise; k-means cannot.

With each program's years shuffled (pure noise), STEM finds 1 significant shape of 50 on the Corning-like mock (29 programs) and 0 on the MS-like mock. On the real mocks: 7 shapes (329 of 778 programs) and 5 (584 of 1,404). K-means on the same noise still returns 10 and 5 clusters, with silhouettes 0.184 and 0.242 against 0.244 and 0.377 on the real mocks.

Pre-registered pass bar (0–1 shapes on noise) met on both mocks. In the paper's own simulation (20 seeds) STEM recovered the 3 planted shapes every time with no false positives, and 2 of 20 pure-noise runs gave one false shape.

Finding 2

Reading budget as a second measurement puts more programs on their true shape.

Exploratory (not pre-registered): giving STEM the budget as a same-year replicate of the actual raises the programs in real shapes from 329 to 477 (Corning-like) and 584 to 830 (MS-like). The share of those sitting on their noise-free shape rises from 0.31 to 0.47 and from 0.75 to 0.82.

Noise-free true levels put 756 of 778 and 1,193 of 1,404 programs in real shapes: observation noise hides most of the structure.

Finding 3

It finds planted groups only partly, and k-means with the same k does as well.

Best F1 against the planted co-moving groups: STEM beats silhouette-chosen k-means (k = 2) by ≥ 0.05 on 3 of 4 Corning-like groups and 1 of 3 MS-like groups (pre-registered pass), e.g. display tradeoff 0.49 vs 0.18, equity activity 0.48 vs 0.24. Given the same number of clusters as STEM, k-means matches or beats it on 5 of 7. Segment × function cells beat both on 5 of 7 (the generator places groups by archetype).

The late-rising AI-demand group is not one of STEM's real shapes (F1 0.18). Power: a shape needs about 80 programs (10% of the book) at noise σ = 0.10 to be detected 90% of the time; 40 programs, 5%.

Finding 4

It does not flag 'says growth, actually declining' better than a slope.

Among programs stated as grow or transform, membership of a real falling shape catches 3 of 6 planted mismatches with 68 flags (Corning-like) and 3 of 25 with 7 flags (MS-like). The most negative OLS slopes, same number of flags, catch 4 and 5.

Pre-registered bar (precision ≥ OLS at matched count) failed on both mocks.

How to read the main chart (the shape grid)
  1. Each of the 50 boxes is one shape fixed in advance (thick line). Thin grey lines are the programs assigned to it (up to 60 drawn).
  2. "69 vs 14" in a corner: 69 programs landed on this shape, 14 would be expected if each program's years were shuffled.
  3. Coloured boxes are over-full beyond chance (Bonferroni 5% over 50 shapes); boxes of the same colour are merged into one cluster of similar shapes.
  4. The pattern to see: a handful of coloured shapes hold hundreds of programs; the other 43–45 shapes hold about what chance gives. White boxes with many lines are not "groups", just noise with that shape.
What it means for a decision-maker

With five or six years per budget line, most "patterns" you can see are coincidence. This tool lists the few spending shapes that occur more often than coincidence allows and says how many lines follow each, so a reviewer can start from the 5–7 real shapes instead of 1,000 lines. It will not find small groups (fewer than about 80 lines), and it does not judge whether a line's description matches its numbers. On these synthetic books, treating the budget as a second reading of the spend made the picture noticeably sharper.

Where it breaks (seen in our runs)

  • Small groups vanish. At σ = 0.10 a planted shape shared by 40 of 778 programs was detected in 1 of 20 runs; 80 programs, 18 of 20.
  • Shape, not size. Correlation ignores how big a move is, so flat noisy programs are forced onto some shape (the white boxes).
  • Shared trends. Raking the mocks to public totals adds common year effects; those make some shapes "real" for reasons unrelated to program behaviour.
  • Home-field reproduction partial. On G27 the filter keeps 2,137 genes, not the printed 2,243 (outside our ±1% bar), although the significant-shape count (10) and, at \(c=1\), the 1,255 genes in significant shapes match.
  • Tooling. stem.jar needs an X display even in batch mode; it ran on the box's display.

What we'd do next

  • Text enrichment per real shape (top pick).
  • A replicate-aware assignment that uses budget and actual jointly (Math avenue).
  • Rerun on real program data when it exists; until then on a new-seed mock.

Well established

What the home field has established

  • Origin. Ernst, Nau & Bar-Joseph (2005, Bioinformatics 21 Suppl. 1:i159–i168) introduced the fixed-shape clustering and permutation test; the search index lists 517 citations for it. The paper text was not read (journal page timed out); its supporting site was read: the simulation page (no significant profiles on pure noise; the three planted profiles recovered with no false positives), the robustness page (9 or 10 of 50 profiles significant for \(c=1,2,3\) on G27; 2,243 genes after filtering; 1,255 / 1,306 / 1,352 in significant profiles) and the k-means comparison page (k = 2 by silhouette gave no more-enriched GO category).
  • Software. Ernst & Bar-Joseph (2006, BMC Bioinformatics 7:191; read): Java tool with GO enrichment, cross-condition comparison and a k-means option; it notes that about 80% of GEO time-series sets have 8 or fewer points. STEM 1.3.15, GPL-3 per its home page; source on GitHub (jernst98/STEM_DREM, 21 stars). Maintained by the original lab; a companion tool (DREM) builds on it.
  • Our reproduction. Simulation design from the supplement, 20 seeds: the 3 planted shapes recovered with 0 false positives in 20 of 20; pure noise gave 0 shapes in 18 of 20 and 1 in 2 of 20. G27 data shipped with STEM, sample settings, \(c=1,2,3\): 10, 10, 10 significant shapes (printed: 9 or 10); genes in significant shapes 1,255 / 1,270 / 1,317 (printed 1,255 / 1,306 / 1,352); genes after filtering 2,137 (printed 2,243, −4.7%, outside our ±1% bar; allowing one missing value gives 2,158). The 2005 numbers came from an earlier STEM version.
  • Known failure modes. Correlation ignores amplitude; the exchangeable-time-points null is wrong when all units share a trend; the binomial test treats units as independent; small groups need many members to show.

Verdict: well established. Two decades of use in its field, a simple and fully specified test, a maintained reference tool, and its published simulation and main real-data count reproduce. Most formal validation is by the originating group, and the filter count did not match the printed one.

1 · Engineering · Effort S
A display-free STEM core

stem.jar needs an X display even in batch mode and enumerates permutations in Java loops. A NumPy core (Rust if it grows) does the assignment and the exact all-orderings expected counts as matrix products, so STEM can run inside the lab's pipelines on thousands of programs.

Math sketch. Centre and scale each unit and each shape once: \(\tilde v_g,\tilde p_j\). For an ordering \(\pi\), \(\rho(v_g^\pi,p_j)=\tilde v_{g\pi}^{\top}\tilde p_j\), so one \((N\times T)\cdot(T\times m)\) product per ordering gives every correlation; \(T!\le 720\) products of a \(10^4\times 6\) by \(6\times 50\) matrix is about \(2\times 10^9\) multiply-adds (computed).

Why it matters here. Real program books will be re-run every planning cycle and with many cuts (segment, function, region); a GUI tool is the bottleneck.

Closest prior work: STEM itself (Java, GPL-3); our NumPy port in runs/2026-11-18/stem/stem_port.py already matches the jar's counts and expected counts.

Test (pass bar set now). Counts, expected counts and significant shapes identical to stem.jar on G27 and both mocks; 10,000 synthetic programs × 6 years with all 720 orderings in under 5 s on the box.

2 · Math · Effort M
Replicate-aware assignment

Budget and actual are two noisy readings of one true level. Instead of averaging them, assign each program by its posterior over shapes, and keep the shuffle null by shuffling both readings with the same ordering.

Math sketch. \(a_{gt}=\mu_{gt}+\varepsilon_{gt}\), \(b_{gt}=\mu_{gt}+\beta+\eta_{gt}\) with \(\mathrm{sd}(\eta)\approx 0.7\,\mathrm{sd}(\varepsilon)\) (the mock's stated noise model). Under shape \(j\), \(\mu_g-\mu_{g0}=\lambda_g p_j\) with \(\lambda_g>0\); integrate \(\lambda_g\) out to get \(P(j\mid a_g,b_g)\), and count \(n_j=\sum_g P(j\mid\cdot)\). The expected count uses the same posterior under orderings.

Why it matters here. Exploratory today: simply passing budget as a STEM replicate already raised the share of programs on their true shape from 0.31 to 0.47 (Corning-like).

Closest prior work: STEM's repeat handling (median of repeats; correlation filter between repeats), read in the manual and source. No posterior-assignment version found.

Test (pass bar set now). On the Corning-like mock, share of programs in real shapes that sit on their noise-free shape ≥ 0.55 (0.47 with the plain replicate), with ≤ 1 real shape on the shuffled copy; on the MS-like mock ≥ 0.85 (0.82 now).

3 · New use on our data · Effort S · ★ Top pick
Text enrichment per real shape (Q2.2, Q2.4)

STEM's main use at home is "which gene categories are over-represented among genes with this shape". Do the same with program descriptions and stated labels: for each real shape, which terms, functions or stated intents are over-represented, and which programs follow the shape although their text says otherwise.

Math sketch. For term \(k\) present in \(M_k\) of \(N\) programs and shape \(j\) holding \(n_j\), the count \(X_{jk}\) of programs with both is hypergeometric under no association: \(P(X_{jk}\ge x)=\sum_{i\ge x}\binom{M_k}{i}\binom{N-M_k}{n_j-i}/\binom{N}{n_j}\). Correct over terms × shapes by Bonferroni or STEM's randomisation method (shuffle shape labels).

Why it matters here. It links the two halves of Q2 (text and numbers) at the level of shapes, which is what a reviewer reads, and it is cheap.

Closest prior work: STEM's GO enrichment (actual-size and expected-size hypergeometric tests; Ernst & Bar-Joseph 2006, read).

Test (pass bar set now). On both mocks, for every planted group whose text reveals it (text_reveals_cluster), its archetype term is enriched in at least one real shape at Bonferroni p < 0.01; on the shuffled copies, at most one term-shape pair passes Bonferroni 5%.

Q2.3 · Real change or noise

STEM separates shapes that occur more than chance from coincidence: 7 of 50 (Corning-like) and 5 of 50 (MS-like) are real; with each program's years shuffled, 1 and 0. Noise hides most structure: 329 of 778 programs sit in real shapes on actuals, 756 on noise-free levels. Reading budget as a second measurement recovers part of it (477; share on the true shape 0.31 → 0.47).

It finds some planted co-moving groups (display tradeoff F1 0.49, equity activity 0.48) but not the late-rising AI-demand group (0.18), and k-means given the same number of clusters does as well on 5 of 7 groups. Groups below about 10% of the book are not detectable at this noise level.
