The model and the statistic

Fit Taylor's power law across units on the log scale (ordinary least squares):

$$\log v_i = \log a + z\,\log m_i + \varepsilon_i .$$

Plugging a size \(m\) into the fitted law gives the CV that one unit of that size would have:

$$\mathrm{CV}(m) = \frac{\sqrt{a\,m^{z}}}{m} = \sqrt{a}\; m^{\,z/2-1}.$$

The mean-variance portfolio effect compares that single-unit CV at the portfolio's size \(M\) with the portfolio's observed CV; the classic average-CV effect compares the average unit CV instead:

$$\mathrm{PE}_{mv} = \frac{\mathrm{CV}(M)}{\mathrm{CV}(X)},\qquad \mathrm{PE}_{avgCV} = \frac{\tfrac1n\sum_i \mathrm{CV}_i}{\mathrm{CV}(X)} .$$

\(\mathrm{PE} > 1\) means the portfolio is steadier than the benchmark. We used the package's linear_detrended variant: each series is detrended before \(v_i\) and \(\mathrm{CV}(X)\) are computed, because budgets trend.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · the size law sets the benchmarkFrom \(\mathrm{CV}(m)=\sqrt a\,m^{z/2-1}\): if \(z=2\), the exponent is 0 and \(\mathrm{CV}(m)=\sqrt a\) for every size. A unit's jumpiness does not depend on how big it is.
Step 2 · when z = 2 the two measures agreeEach \(\mathrm{CV}_i\) is then (on the fitted line) \(\sqrt a\), so \(\tfrac1n\sum_i \mathrm{CV}_i = \sqrt a = \mathrm{CV}(M)\) and \(\mathrm{PE}_{avgCV} = \mathrm{PE}_{mv}\). The average-CV measure is the special case that silently assumes \(z = 2\).
Step 3 · when z < 2 the average-CV measure overstatesThe exponent \(z/2-1\) is negative, so \(\mathrm{CV}(m)\) falls with size. The portfolio is much bigger than any single program (\(M \gg m_i\)), so \(\mathrm{CV}(M) < \mathrm{CV}(m_i)\) for every \(i\), hence \(\mathrm{CV}(M) < \tfrac1n\sum_i \mathrm{CV}_i\) and \(\mathrm{PE}_{avgCV} > \mathrm{PE}_{mv}\). Part of what looks like diversification is just "big things are calmer".
Step 4 · what remains is co-movement\(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sum_i v_i + \sum_{i\ne j}\mathrm{Cov}(x_i,x_j)\). If units moved independently the covariance sum would vanish and \(\mathrm{CV}(X)\) would be small; positive co-movement pushes \(\mathrm{CV}(X)\) up and \(\mathrm{PE}_{mv}\) down toward (or below) 1.

Inference and the null

  • Interval: the package's interval covers only the uncertainty in the Taylor fit \((a,z)\), not the sampling error of \(\mathrm{CV}(X)\). We report it as such (Corning mock: 0.99 [0.74, 1.31]).
  • Null for "is there co-movement?": shuffle each program's years independently (999 times). This keeps every \(m_i\) and \(v_i\) (so \(a, z\) are unchanged) and destroys the covariance term in Step 4. Observed 0.99 vs null median 7.28 (Corning mock), \(p = 0.001\).
  • Fragility check: refit with a robust line and a quadratic in \(\log m\); leave one year out for \(z\) (1.62–1.72 on Corning mock).
Toy example (computed on the box)

Three programs of mean size \(m = 1, 4, 16\) on a law with \(a = 0.04,\ z = 1.5\). Their CVs are \(\sqrt{0.04}\,m^{-0.25} = 0.200,\ 0.141,\ 0.100\); average 0.147. The portfolio has \(M = 21\), so one program that big would have \(\mathrm{CV}(21) = 0.2 \times 21^{-0.25} = 0.093\). Suppose the observed portfolio CV is 0.060.

\(\mathrm{PE}_{avgCV} = 0.147/0.060 = 2.45\) ("2.5× diversified") but \(\mathrm{PE}_{mv} = 0.093/0.060 = 1.56\). Of the apparent gain, the part from 1 to 1.56 is diversification; the part from 1.56 to 2.45 is size.

How it differs from the standard tool. The finance diversification ratio \(\sum_i\sigma_i/\sigma_X\) and the average-CV effect both compare the portfolio with its own parts; neither asks how volatile one unit of the portfolio's size would be. The mean-variance effect adds one regression (the size law) and changes the benchmark, not the data. With \(z\) estimated at 1.67 / 1.77 instead of 2, that one change moves the headline from about 3× to about 1×.

Followed: Anderson, Cooper & Dulvy (2013), Methods in Ecology and Evolution 4:971–981 (mean-variance portfolio effect; read via the DOI summary, so section and equation numbers were not checked); Taylor (1961); Cohen & Xu (2015) PNAS 112:7749 for why the law arises. Inputs and preprocessing

  • SYNTHETIC zero-based-budgeting mocks (seed 20261113): 1,079 programs (Corning mock) and 1,840 (MS mock). The answer key was used only for scoring.
  • Balanced panel only, because the package needs every program in every year: 778 of 1,079 and 1,404 of 1,840 programs kept (28% and 24% dropped).
  • Each series linearly detrended before computing variances (linear_detrended).

What we ran

  1. Reproduced the package vignette on pink salmon (pinkbr): PEmv 1.412 and PEavgCV 1.517, exactly as published.
  2. Fitted \(z\) per firm (1.67 Corning mock, 1.77 MS mock) and per segment; computed PEmv, PEavgCV and the finance diversification ratio.
  3. Robustness: linear / robust / quadratic Taylor fits; leave-one-year-out \(z\); the same on noise-free true levels from the answer key.
  4. Null: 999 year-shuffles for the whole book; 299 random same-size sets for every subsegment, driver and function group, to see if PE finds planted co-moving groups.
  5. Per-program flag: residual from the Taylor line vs plain CV, scored by AUC against planted "movers".
How to read the main chart (Taylor plot, first finding's original chart)
  1. Each dot is one mock program: x = log mean spend, y = log variance.
  2. The solid line is the fitted size law; its slope is \(z\) (1.67 on the Corning mock), flatter than the dashed \(z = 2\) line.
  3. Both lines are extended far right to the book's size (2.1 decades past the largest program).
  4. The red dot is the real book. The gap between the dashed line and the red dot is what the usual measure calls diversification; the gap between the solid line and the red dot is what is left after allowing for size (about none).
What it means for a decision-maker

"Our program book is three times steadier than its programs" is mostly a statement about size, not about programs offsetting each other. A single program as big as the whole book would be about as steady. Do not count on diversification inside the book to absorb a bad year; the programs move together (the shuffle test says far more than chance), partly because the mock is built to add up to public totals.

Where it breaks (seen in our runs)

  • Extrapolation: the benchmark sits about two orders of magnitude beyond the data. The quadratic fit blew up (34.7 vs 0.83 linear on the Corning mock).
  • Short series: 3–4 degrees of freedom per variance. Per-program flags failed (AUC 0.28–0.34 for Taylor residual and CV).
  • Not a group finder: small groups give wild values (up to 283 for a 12-program group) because the within-group fit is poor.
  • Ragged panels: a quarter of programs dropped for entering or exiting.
  • Noise: on noise-free levels both measures fall (Corning mock: average-CV 1.54, mean-variance 0.25), so the reported numbers include noise cancelling in the total.

What we'd do next

The top pick: estimate observation noise from the budget-vs-actual gap and remove it before fitting \(z\) (Secret sauce, card 2), pre-registered against 20 new-seed mocks. Then run it on real program budgets and actuals once we have them.

Promising but thin

What the home field has established

  • Taylor's law is solid. Reported since Taylor (1961) across insects, fish and plants. Cohen & Xu (PNAS 112:7749–7754, 2015; abstract read) show that random block sampling from any skewed distribution with four finite moments produces it, with formulae for the slope and its standard error. A follow-up exchange (doi:10.1073/pnas.1507551112) disputed when samples support it.
  • The portfolio metric is newer and thinner. It rests on one methods paper (Anderson, Cooper & Dulvy 2013; 88 citations in DOI metadata). From its summary: across 51 metapopulations and 1,070 subpopulations the average-CV effect was stronger than the mean-variance one in 84% of cases, by up to sevenfold; the gap was largest for low-\(z\) reef fishes and moths and vanished for salmon (\(z \approx 2\)).
  • Package: ecofolio 0.1.0, single author, no test suite, last push 2025-06. Our reproduction of its vignette matched exactly; the core is simple enough to verify by hand (we did, to 1e-15).
  • Known failure modes: long extrapolation of the single-unit variance; the package interval ignores the uncertainty of the portfolio CV.

Grade from the 2026-11-16 edition: "promising but thin. The scaling law is solid. The portfolio-effect metric has one primary paper and an unmaintained, untested package."

1 · Engineering · Effort S–M
Ragged-panel port, R as oracle

ecofolio needs a complete program × year matrix, so 24–28% of programs were dropped. A NumPy/Rust port could keep entrants and exiters.

Math sketch. Replace the per-program variance with an unbiased variance on each program's observed years (detrended within its own span), weight the Taylor regression by degrees of freedom, \(\min_{a,z}\sum_i (n_i-2)\,(\log v_i - \log a - z\log m_i)^2\), and compute \(\mathrm{CV}(X)\) on years where the total is defined.

Test. Parity ≤ 1e-10 with ecofolio on pinkbr and on 1,000 random balanced subsets; then a 999-shuffle null on 1,404 programs in < 1 s (today's vectorised R: 0.1 ms per PE vs 215 ms in the package).

2 · Math · Effort M · ★ Top pick
A noise-corrected Taylor's law and portfolio effect

Budget and actual are two noisy readings of each program. Their disagreement estimates observation-noise variance, which can be removed before fitting \(z\). Then split variability across program → segment → firm (α/β/γ).

Math sketch. If \(b_{it} = \theta_{it} + \delta_{it}\) and \(x_{it} = \theta_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\) with independent errors, \(\mathrm{Var}(x_{it}-b_{it}) = \sigma^2_\epsilon + \sigma^2_\delta\). With the mocks' stated noise ratio \(r = \sigma_\delta/\sigma_\epsilon\), \(\hat\sigma^2_{\epsilon,i} = \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(x_i-b_i)/(1+r^2)\), and the corrected variance is \(\tilde v_i = \max(v_i - \hat\sigma^2_{\epsilon,i}, 0)\). Fit Taylor on \(\tilde v_i\), then partition total variability as in Wang & Loreau (2014). Today noise inflated the average-CV effect from 1.54 to 3.36.

Test. On both mocks plus 20 new-seed mocks: the corrected PE is closer to the true-level PE in ≥ 80% of firm × segment cells. Pre-registered.
