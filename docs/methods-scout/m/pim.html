The model

$$P\big(Y_i \preccurlyeq Y_j \mid X_i, X_j\big) = \operatorname{expit}\!\big((X_j - X_i)^\top \beta\big),\qquad (i,j)\in\mathcal I .$$

The left side is the probabilistic index (PI): the chance that bank \(j\), which is higher by \(X_j - X_i\) on the stress metric, has the higher payout. \(\beta = 0\) gives PI = 0.5, "the metric does not order payouts". Nothing is assumed about the shape of \(Y\)'s distribution: only this pairwise probability is modelled (semiparametric).

The estimator

Make one pseudo-observation per pair, \(I_{ij} = \mathbf 1[Y_i < Y_j] + \tfrac12\mathbf 1[Y_i = Y_j]\) with design \(Z_{ij} = X_j - X_i\), and solve the estimating equation

$$U(\beta)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal I} Z_{ij}\Big(I_{ij} - \operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\beta)\Big) = 0 .$$

That is exactly the score of a logistic regression on the pairs, so it is fast (49,500 pairs fit in 0.05 s). But the pairs share banks, so they are not independent: the variance needs a sandwich that accounts for the overlap.

Why it works, step by step

Step 1 · unbiased estimating equationUnder the model, \(\mathbb E[I_{ij}\mid X_i,X_j] = \operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\beta)\), so each term of \(U(\beta)\) has mean zero at the true \(\beta\). Solving \(U(\hat\beta)=0\) is consistent even though the pairs are dependent.
Step 2 · it contains the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney testWith one binary covariate (group A = 0, B = 1) every cross-group pair has \(Z_{ij} = 1\), and \(U(\beta)=0\) gives \(\operatorname{expit}(\hat\beta) = \tfrac1{n_An_B}\sum_{i\in A,\,j\in B} I_{ij}\): the Mann–Whitney probability. A PIM is "the rank test turned into a regression"; with more covariates it gives partial effects (drawdown with pre-tax income held fixed).
Step 3 · converting to "per IQR"Because the link is on the difference, a gap of \(\Delta\) in the metric gives PI \(=\operatorname{expit}(\beta\Delta)\). Our pre-tax income fit has PI 0.606 per 1 pp, so \(\beta = \operatorname{logit}(0.606) = 0.431\), and at the IQR \(\Delta = 1.8\) pp: \(\operatorname{expit}(0.431\times1.8) = 0.685\), the 68% on the card. Drawdown: \(\operatorname{expit}(\operatorname{logit}(0.460)\times2.5) = 0.401\).
Step 4 · why outliers barely matterEach pair contributes \(I_{ij}\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\). Moving one bank's payout from 5% to 500% of RWA changes nothing unless it changes who beats whom. OLS instead squares the distance, so a near-zero net income can dominate.

Inference and the null

  • Pim's sandwich treats firm-years as independent, but firms recur across 13 years. We used a firm-block bootstrap (299 resamples of whole firms) for intervals and a within-year permutation of \(Y\) (499) for p-values. Within-year permutation keeps each year's payout norms and breaks the link to \(X\).
  • Small samples (18–35 banks a year) are where Amorim et al. (2018) found asymptotic PIM inference unreliable, which is why we lean on resampling.
  • 29 models in three families; Holm within each family.
Toy example (computed on the box)

Two banks with a weak stress result (A) paid 1.0 and 2.0; three with a strong result (B) paid 1.5, 3.0 and 4.0. Of the 6 A–B pairs, B pays more in 5 (only 2.0 > 1.5 goes the other way). So \(\widehat{PI} = 5/6 = 0.83\) and \(\hat\beta = \operatorname{logit}(0.83) = 1.61\). Change the 4.0 to 400: still 5 of 6, PI unchanged. An OLS slope would jump.

Firm-clustered variance (Math avenue, 2026-11-17)

Pim's default sandwich counts two pairs as dependent only when they share a firm-year. In our panel the same bank appears in up to 13 years, and both its stress results and its payout habits persist from year to year. So two pairs that share a firm, even in different years, are also dependent, and the default error is too small.

Step 1 · pair scoresEach within-year pair \(p=(i,j)\) contributes \(u_p = Z_{ij}(I_{ij}-\operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\hat\beta))\), and \(U=\sum_p u_p = 0\) at \(\hat\beta\). Swapping the order of the pair leaves \(u_p\) unchanged.
Step 2 · the meat counts every pair of pairs that shares a firm, once\(B_F=\sum_f S_fS_f^\top-\sum_{\{a,b\}}V_{ab}V_{ab}^\top\), where \(S_f=\sum_{p\ni f}u_p\) and \(V_{ab}\) sums the pairs, in any year, made of firms \(a\) and \(b\). Two pairs that share one firm appear once in \(\sum_f S_fS_f^\top\). Two that share both firms (the same two banks in different years, or a pair with itself) appear twice, and the second term removes the extra copy.
Step 3 · sandwich\(\hat V_F = \tfrac{G}{G-1}A^{-1}B_FA^{-1}\) with \(A=\sum_p w_pZ_pZ_p^\top\), where \(w_p=\hat\pi_p(1-\hat\pi_p)\) and G = 40 banks. Replace firm IDs with firm-year IDs and set the factor to 1, and you get pim's own sandwich exactly. We checked this: both reproduce pim 2.0.4's errors to 7e-15.
Step 4 · what it does not fixWith only 40 clusters the meat is still estimated from few independent units, and Amorim et al.'s small-sample bias reduction is not in this version. In simulation the new errors are about 9% too small (Monte Carlo SD / mean SE = 1.09 and 1.10), which gives the 91.7% coverage below.
Toy example (computed on the box)

Banks A, B and C in two years, one covariate. Pair scores: year 1 AB 0.20, AC −0.10, BC 0.30; year 2 AB 0.25, AC −0.05, BC 0.20. The firm-year meat (pim) is 0.16 per year, 0.32 in total. The firm meat is \(S=(0.30, 0.95, 0.35)\), so \(\sum S^2=1.115\), minus \(\sum V^2 = 0.45^2+0.15^2+0.50^2=0.475\), which leaves 0.64. That is twice as large, because the same banks' pairs in the two years now count as dependent.

Simulation result (pre-registered bar: 93.5–96.5% coverage in both scenarios). 40 banks × 13 years with 18–35 observed each year, and firm effects in both \(x\) and \(y\); R = 2,000 runs. β = 0: pim sandwich 72.3%, firm-clustered 91.7%, firm-block bootstrap 95.0% (500 runs). β = 0.5 (pseudo-truth 0.411 from 400-bank fits): 72.0%, 91.8%, 93.2%. Miss: a large repair, but not enough. Within-year permutation p-values (used on 11-16) face the same problem, since they also break the across-year firm link. This was not tested here.

Code: runs/2026-11-17/pim2/pimfast.py (estimator), math_sim.py, math_apply.py. Pre-registration: runs/2026-11-17/PREREG.md section E2.

How it differs from the standard tool. OLS with year fixed effects models \(\mathbb E[Y\mid X]\) and reports a slope in payout units; with heavy tails it is driven by a few extreme ratios (−43% after trimming 2% of rows). A PIM models only the ordering within a year and reports a probability. Compared with the 11-14 IDR (full conditional distributions under an order constraint), it gives a partial effect size, at the cost of saying nothing about levels.

Followed: Thas, De Neve, Clement & Ottoy (2012), JRSS-B 74(4):623–671 (model and estimating equations; abstract read, section and equation numbers not checked); Amorim et al. (2018), CSDA 121:137–148 (small-sample bias). Package pim 2.0.4; our check reproduced the WMW special case exactly (PI 0.749275 on FEVData). Toy: cards/_toys/toys.py.

How to read the main chart (forest plot)
  1. Each row is one model; the dot is the PI per one-IQR difference in the stress metric.
  2. The vertical line at 0.5 means "no ordering". Right of it: the better-placed bank tends to pay more.
  3. Bars are firm-block bootstrap 95% intervals; a bar that crosses 0.5 is not distinguishable from no ordering.

What it means for a decision-maker

Within a stress-test year, the bank whose severely adverse projected pre-tax income looks better usually pays out more: about 2 times in 3 for a typical gap. The capital drawdown on its own seems to matter only because it travels with pre-tax income. The buffer itself (SCB) shows no link. This is a description of how payouts lined up with published stress numbers, not advice on setting payouts and not a forecast.

Where it breaks (seen in our runs)

  • Collinearity: SCB + drawdown together gave unstable, sign-flipping coefficients (Spearman 0.92 between them).
  • Small yearly cross-sections (18–35 banks): the package's sandwich p-values are optimistic; we used resampling instead.
  • Ties: many zero buybacks; handled as ½, but they dilute the buyback leg.
  • Timing: payouts in calendar \(Y\) partly precede year-\(Y\) results; next-year checks were weaker (next-year dividend share vs drawdown: PI 0.46 per IQR, p = 0.17).
  • Not available: firm-level baseline projections, so "versus baseline" cannot be asked.

Well established

What the home field has established

  • Origin. A semiparametric model for \(P(Y \le Y^* \mid X, X^*)\) with asymptotic normality of the estimator, a consistent sandwich covariance and a simulation study (Thas et al., JRSS-B 2012; abstract read). DOI metadata lists 163 citations.
  • Follow-ups. Amorim et al. (2018; abstract and introduction read) describe PIMs as generating classical rank tests (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, Friedman). They show asymptotic inference is poor in small samples and \(\beta\) has a large finite-sample bias; their bias-reduced bootstrap and adjusted jackknife empirical likelihood fix it.
  • Implementation. pim 2.0.4 on CRAN (2025-01), tests in Suggests, maintained by the Ghent group. Our reproduction: PI = 0.749275 on FEVData, identical to WMW.
  • Known failure modes. Small samples, dependent pseudo-observations, ties, collinear covariates. All four showed up here.

Grade from the 2026-11-16 edition: "well established as a statistical framework with theory, simulations and a maintained package. It is little used outside biostatistics and nearly unknown in finance."

1 · Engineering · Effort M
A streaming within-block PIM fitter

With within-year pairs, the score is a sum over small blocks. A Rust core could batch permutation and firm-bootstrap refits without rebuilding pair matrices, and scale to thousands of Q2 programs per subsegment. pim 2.0.4 is the oracle.

Math sketch. \(U(\beta)=\sum_{y}\sum_{i<j\in y} Z_{ij}(I_{ij}-\operatorname{expit}(Z_{ij}^\top\beta))\). A permutation within year \(y\) only re-labels \(I_{ij}\); the \(Z_{ij}\) blocks are fixed, so Newton steps can reuse \(\sum Z_{ij}Z_{ij}^\top w_{ij}\) structure block by block.

Test. β and SE parity ≤ 1e-8 with pim on all 29 models; then 10,000 within-year permutations of a 2-covariate model on 360 firm-years in < 2 s.

2 · Math · Effort M
Firm-clustered inference for PIMs

Firms repeat across test years, but pim's sandwich treats firm-years as independent. Derive a two-way (firm × year) U-statistic variance with Amorim et al.'s small-sample bias reduction.

Math sketch. \(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta) = A^{-1} B A^{-1}\) with \(A = -\partial U/\partial\beta\) and \(B = \sum_{f}\sum_{f'} \mathrm{cov}(U_f, U_{f'})\), where \(U_f\) collects every pair that involves firm \(f\); overlap between \(U_f\) and \(U_{f'}\) comes from the pair \((f,f')\) itself.

Test. Simulation at our design (40 firms × 13 years, 18–35 per year, firm effects): 95% coverage within ±1.5 points, against the sandwich and the block bootstrap.
